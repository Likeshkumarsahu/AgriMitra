## Module 12 — Plant Nutrition
Macronutrients
1. Nitrogen (N)
## 1. Introduction
Nitrogen (N) is one of the most important essential mineral nutrients required by plants. It is a
primary macronutrient, along with phosphorus (P) and potassium (K), and is required in relatively
large quantities.
Nitrogen is fundamental because it forms part of:
- amino acids,
- proteins,
- enzymes,
- chlorophyll,
- nucleic acids (DNA and RNA),
- ATP and other energy-transfer compounds,
- many coenzymes and metabolites.
A simplified relationship is:
N→Amino Acids→Proteins/Enzymes→Photosynthesis+Growth+Metabolism 
Nitrogen therefore strongly influences:
- vegetative growth,
- leaf area,
- chlorophyll concentration,
- photosynthesis,
- tillering and branching,
- biomass production,
- yield formation,
- protein content.

But more nitrogen does not automatically mean more yield. Excess N can cause excessive
vegetative growth, lodging, delayed maturity, pest/disease problems and environmental losses.

2. Definition of Nitrogen
Nitrogen is an essential primary macronutrient required by plants for the synthesis of
amino acids, proteins, nucleic acids, chlorophyll, enzymes and numerous other
biological compounds, making it essential for plant growth, photosynthesis,
metabolism and yield formation.

Exam-ready definition
Nitrogen is an essential plant macronutrient that forms a structural and functional
component of amino acids, proteins, nucleic acids, chlorophyll and several metabolic
compounds, thereby regulating plant growth, photosynthesis, biomass production and
yield.

5. Nitrogen and Amino Acids
Amino acids are the building blocks of proteins.
Plants absorb inorganic nitrogen and convert it into organic nitrogen compounds.
A simplified pathway is:
NO3−/NH4+→NH4+→Amino Acids→Proteins 
Important amino acids include:
- glutamate,
- glutamine,
- aspartate,
- asparagine.

6. Nitrogen and Proteins
Proteins are required for:
- cell structure,
- enzymes,

- transport,
- metabolism,
- photosynthesis,
- stress responses.
Therefore:
N deficiency→Protein synthesis↓→Growth and metabolism↓ 

7. Nitrogen and Chlorophyll
Nitrogen is an important component of chlorophyll molecules.
Chlorophyll allows plants to absorb light for photosynthesis.
Therefore:
N→Chlorophyll→Light Capture→Photosynthesis 
Nitrogen deficiency commonly causes leaf chlorosis, particularly in older leaves.

8. Nitrogen and Photosynthesis
Adequate N supports:
- chlorophyll,
- photosynthetic proteins,
- electron transport components,
- photosynthetic enzymes,
- leaf area.
Thus:
N availability→Leaf N→Photosynthetic Capacity 
But excessive nitrogen does not indefinitely increase photosynthesis.
There is an optimum range.

9. Nitrogen and Nucleic Acids
Nitrogen is present in nitrogenous bases of:
- DNA,
- RNA.
These molecules are required for:
- genetic information storage,
- transcription,
- translation,
- cell division,
- protein synthesis.
Therefore nitrogen is directly connected with plant growth at the cellular level.

10. Nitrogen and Enzymes
Many enzymes are proteins.
Therefore nitrogen is indirectly essential for virtually every major metabolic pathway.
N→Protein→Enzyme→Metabolic Reaction 

11. Nitrogen and Plant Growth
Nitrogen strongly promotes:
- leaf expansion,
- shoot growth,
- stem growth,
- tillering,
- branching,
- canopy development.
Adequate N generally produces:
- vigorous plants,

- larger green leaves,
- greater biomass.
However:
Too Little N→Poor Growth 
and:
Too Much N→Excessive Vegetative Growth 
The goal is adequate, balanced N nutrition.

12. Nitrogen and Root Growth
Nitrogen affects root growth indirectly through overall plant carbon and nutrient balance.
Moderate adequate N supports healthy root development.
However, excessive N can favor shoot growth disproportionately in some conditions:
Excess N→Shoot Growth↑→Root:Shoot Ratio↓ 
Therefore high N does not necessarily mean a stronger root system.

shoot growth.
Plant responses depend on:
- crop,

- developmental stage,
- soil fertility,
- water availability,
- other nutrients.

14. Nitrogen and Yield
Nitrogen influences yield through several components.
For a cereal:
Yield≈Plants/Area×Productive Units/Plant×Grains/Unit×Grain Weight 
Nitrogen can influence:
- tiller/branch production,
- reproductive development,
- grain/seed number,
- grain/seed filling,
- protein content.
But the response is not unlimited.

16. Forms of Nitrogen
Plants primarily acquire nitrogen from soil solution as:
NO3− 
and:
NH4+ 
These are the major mineral forms directly available for plant uptake.

Plants can also acquire small amounts of organic N compounds in certain systems, but nitrate and
ammonium are the dominant forms considered in conventional plant nutrition.

17. Nitrate Nitrogen
Nitrate:
NO3− 
is highly mobile in soil.

Characteristics
- readily transported with soil water,
- important plant N source,
- prone to leaching,
- can be lost through denitrification under suitable anaerobic conditions.
Because nitrate is an anion and soil particles generally have predominantly negative charges, it is
usually less strongly retained than cations such as K+K +, Ca2+Ca  2+  and NH4+NH 4 +.

18. Ammonium Nitrogen
Ammonium:
NH4+ 
is a positively charged ion.
It can be:
- absorbed directly by roots,
- held on cation-exchange sites,
- converted to nitrate through nitrification,
- incorporated into organic compounds,
- lost as ammonia under certain surface conditions.

20. Nitrogen Cycle
Nitrogen constantly moves among:
- atmosphere,
- soil,
- microorganisms,
- plants,
- water.
The agricultural nitrogen cycle can be represented as:
N2↔NH3/NH4+↔NO2−↔NO3−↔Organic N 
Important processes include:
1. nitrogen fixation,
2. mineralization,
3. ammonification,
4. nitrification,
5. plant uptake,
6. immobilization,
7. denitrification,
8. leaching,

9. volatilization.

21. Atmospheric Nitrogen
The atmosphere contains approximately:
∼78%N2 
by volume.
However, atmospheric N2N 2 is relatively unavailable to most plants because of its strong triple bond:
N≡N 
Plants generally cannot directly use atmospheric N2N 2 in this form.

22. Biological Nitrogen Fixation
Certain microorganisms can convert atmospheric nitrogen into biologically usable forms.
Conceptually:
N2→NH3→NH4+→Organic N 
Important nitrogen-fixing organisms include:
- Rhizobium and related rhizobia,
- Bradyrhizobium,
- Azotobacter,
- Azospirillum,
- cyanobacteria in appropriate environments.

23. Symbiotic Nitrogen Fixation
Legumes form symbiotic associations with rhizobia.
Examples:
- chickpea,
- pea,

- lentil,
- soybean,
- pigeon pea,
- groundnut.
The bacteria live in root nodules.
Simplified pathway:
Legume↔Rhizobia→Biological N Fixation 
The plant supplies carbon/energy to the bacteria, while the symbiosis provides biologically fixed
nitrogen to the plant.

24. Root Nodules
Root nodules are specialized structures associated with nitrogen-fixing symbioses.
A simplified sequence is:
Root Recognition→Bacterial Infection→Nodule Formation→N2 Fixation 
The enzyme nitrogenase is central to biological nitrogen fixation.

## 25. Nitrogenase
Nitrogenase catalyzes the reduction of atmospheric nitrogen.
Simplified representation:
N2→NH3 
Nitrogenase is highly sensitive to oxygen, so nodules have specialized mechanisms for maintaining an
appropriate oxygen environment.

## 26. Leghemoglobin
In legume nodules, leghemoglobin helps regulate oxygen availability around nitrogen-fixing bacteria.
It contributes to maintaining conditions suitable for nitrogenase activity while supporting respiration.

This is why healthy active nodules often have a pinkish/red interior.

## 27. Mineralization
Mineralization is the conversion of organic nitrogen into inorganic forms that plants can potentially
use.
Simplified:
Organic N→NH4+ 
Microorganisms carry out much of this process.

## 28. Ammonification
Ammonification is commonly used for the microbial conversion of organic nitrogen into ammonium.
Organic N→NH4+ 
It is an important part of nitrogen mineralization.

## 29. Nitrification
Nitrification is the biological oxidation of ammonium.
Simplified:
NH4+→NO2−→NO3− 
Different microbial groups perform the successive steps.
Nitrification generally requires oxygen.

30. Nitrifying Microorganisms
The first step is associated with organisms such as:
- Nitrosomonas and related ammonia-oxidizing organisms.
The second step is associated with organisms such as:
- Nitrobacter and related nitrite-oxidizing organisms.

Modern microbiology recognizes broader groups, including archaea and other bacteria, so these
classical examples should not be interpreted as the only organisms involved.

## 31. Denitrification
Under low-oxygen or anaerobic conditions, microorganisms can reduce nitrate.
A simplified pathway is:
NO3−→NO2−→NO→N2O→N2 
This can result in nitrogen loss to the atmosphere.
Denitrification is favored by conditions such as:
- waterlogging,
- low oxygen,
- available carbon,
- suitable microbial activity.

32. Nitrogen Leaching
Nitrate is highly mobile.
When water moves downward through soil:
NO3−+Downward Water Movement→Leaching 
Consequences include:
- fertilizer-N loss,
- lower nitrogen-use efficiency,
- groundwater contamination risk.
Leaching risk is especially important in:
- sandy soils,
- excessive irrigation,
- heavy rainfall,
- shallow-rooted crops.

33. Ammonia Volatilization
Nitrogen can also be lost as ammonia gas:
NH4+⇌NH3(g)+H+ 
Volatilization risk is particularly important for surface-applied:
- urea,
- ammonium-containing fertilizers,
- manures.
Risk increases under conditions such as:
- high pH,
- warm temperatures,
- moist but drying surfaces,
- surface application without incorporation,
- high carbonate/bicarbonate conditions.

34. Nitrogen Immobilization
Microorganisms require nitrogen to build their biomass.
When microbes decompose carbon-rich residues with a high C:N ratio:
High C:N Residue→Microbial N Immobilization 
This temporarily reduces mineral N available to plants.

36. Nitrogen Uptake by Roots
Plants absorb nitrogen mainly as:

37. Nitrogen Transport to Shoots
After uptake, nitrogen is transported through vascular tissues.
A simplified pathway is:
Soil→Root→Xylem→Shoot/Leaves 
Nitrogen is also redistributed through the phloem from older tissues to developing sinks.

38. Nitrogen Assimilation
Absorbed inorganic nitrogen must be incorporated into organic molecules.
For ammonium, an important pathway involves:
GS/GOGAT 
where:
- GS = glutamine synthetase,
- GOGAT = glutamate synthase.
Simplified:
NH4+→Glutamine→Glutamate→Other Amino AcidsNH 4 +  Glutamine  Glutamate  Other\ Amino\ Acids

39. Why Ammonium Must Be Carefully
Regulated
Although plants can use ammonium, excessive accumulation of free NH4+NH 4 + can be toxic.
Therefore plants rapidly assimilate ammonium into organic compounds.
NH4+→Amino Acid Assimilation 

40. Nitrogen Remobilization
Nitrogen can move from older leaves to developing reproductive organs.
During grain filling:
Older Leaf N→Phloem→Developing Grain 
This process is called nitrogen remobilization.
It contributes significantly to grain and seed N supply.

41. Nitrogen and Source–Sink Relations
The source produces or supplies assimilates and nutrients.

The sink uses or stores them.
Source→Phloem→Sink 
Examples of sinks:
- developing grains,
- seeds,
- fruits,
- tubers,
- roots.
Nitrogen affects both source capacity and sink development.

42. Nitrogen Deficiency
Nitrogen deficiency is one of the most common nutrient deficiencies in crop production.
Typical symptoms include:
- older leaves becoming pale green,
- progressive yellowing/chlorosis,
- reduced growth,
- reduced leaf area,
- thin/small plants,
- reduced tillering or branching,
- premature senescence,
- reduced yield.

43. Why Nitrogen Deficiency Appears First in
Older Leaves
Nitrogen is relatively mobile within plants.
When N becomes limiting, plants can redistribute N from older leaves toward younger, actively
growing tissues.
Therefore:

N deficiency→N remobilization→Older leaves chlorose first 
This is a useful diagnostic principle.

44. Nitrogen Deficiency Symptoms
Visual symptoms
- pale green foliage,
- yellowing,
- older leaves affected first,
- reduced plant size,
- thin stems,
- reduced tillering/branching.

Physiological symptoms
- chlorophyll↓,
- photosynthesis↓,
- protein synthesis↓,
- leaf expansion↓,
- biomass accumulation↓.

Yield effects
- fewer reproductive units,
- smaller grains/seeds/fruits,
- reduced biomass,
- lower yield.

45. Nitrogen Deficiency vs Iron Deficiency
Both can cause chlorosis, but their patterns differ.

Nitrogen deficiency
Usually:
Older leaves first  
because N is mobile.

Iron deficiency
Often:
Young leaves first  
because Fe is relatively immobile in the phloem.
This is an important diagnostic distinction.

46. Nitrogen Toxicity / Excess Nitrogen
Excess N can cause:
- excessive vegetative growth,
- dark green foliage,
- soft/succulent tissues,
- delayed maturity,
- weak stems,
- lodging,
- greater susceptibility to some pests/diseases,
- poor reproductive balance,
- reduced quality in some crops.
Excess N→Vegetative Growth↑→Reproductive Balance↓ 
The exact response is crop- and environment-dependent.

47. Nitrogen and Lodging
Excess nitrogen can produce:
- taller plants,

- greater biomass,
- weaker or less proportionately strong stems in susceptible crops.
Therefore:
Excess N→Lodging Risk↑ 
Lodging can reduce:
- light interception,
- grain filling,
- harvest efficiency,
- final yield.
Plant architecture and potassium nutrition also influence lodging.

48. Nitrogen and Maturity
High N availability can prolong vegetative growth and delay maturity in some crops.
Therefore:
Excess N→Vegetative Duration↑→Maturity Delay 
The response depends on crop and environment.

49. Nitrogen and Water Use
Adequate N can increase:
- leaf area,
- canopy size,
- transpiration,
- biomass.
Therefore N and water are strongly interconnected.
Under drought:
N response≠same as under adequate waterN\ response  Applying large N amounts to a severely water-limited crop may not produce the expected benefit.

50. Nitrogen × Phosphorus
Nitrogen and phosphorus interact strongly.
Phosphorus supports:
- energy transfer,
- root development,
- reproductive processes.
Nitrogen supports:
- proteins,
- chlorophyll,
- leaf growth.
Balanced nutrition is therefore more important than maximizing one nutrient.
Balanced N+P>Excessive N alone 

51. Nitrogen × Potassium
Potassium supports:
- water regulation,
- enzyme activation,
- stomatal function,
- structural and stress-related processes.
Adequate K can support plants receiving N, but K cannot simply “cancel” excessive N.
N×K→Growth+Water Regulation+Stress Response 

52. Nitrogen × Sulfur
Nitrogen and sulfur are particularly connected because both are required for protein synthesis.
Important S-containing amino acids include:
- cysteine,

- methionine.
Therefore:
N deficiency and S deficiency→Protein Synthesis Problems  S\
deficiency  Protein\ Synthesis\ Problems  
High N availability with inadequate S can produce poor N utilization.

53. Nitrogen and Sulfur Balance
A simplified concept is:
N:S Balance→Protein Synthesis→Nitrogen Use Efficiency 
The appropriate ratio varies by crop and tissue.

54. Nitrogen and Soil pH
Nitrogen transformations influence soil pH.
For example, nitrification of ammonium ultimately produces acidity:
NH4+→NO3−+H+ 
Repeated use of ammonium-form N fertilizers can therefore contribute to soil acidification over time,
depending on fertilizer source and overall N cycle.

55. Urea and Nitrogen
Urea is one of the most widely used N fertilizers.
Chemical formula:
CO(NH2)2 
Urea is converted in soil through microbial/enzymatic processes.
Simplified:
Urea→NH4+→NO3− 

56. Urea Hydrolysis
Urea is hydrolyzed by the enzyme urease.
Simplified pathway:
Urea→NH3/NH4+

leading to volatilization losses.

64. Nitrogen Loss by Leaching
Especially important for nitrate:
NO3−+Excess Water→Leaching 
Risk increases with:
- sandy soil,
- excessive irrigation,
- heavy rainfall,
- nitrate accumulation,
- shallow rooting.

65. Nitrogen Loss by Volatilization
Important particularly for surface-applied urea and ammonium fertilizers.
NH4+⇌NH3(g) 
Risk increases with:
- high pH,
- warm conditions,
- surface application,
- carbonate-rich conditions,
- manure/urea on the soil surface.

66. Nitrogen Loss by Denitrification
Under waterlogged/anaerobic conditions:
NO3−→N2O→N2 
This represents loss of reactive nitrogen from the soil system.

67. Nitrogen Loss Through Runoff
Nitrogen can move with:
- dissolved nitrate,
- ammonium,
- organic N,
- eroded soil particles.
Therefore heavy rainfall and poor soil conservation can increase N losses.

68. Nitrogen Management — The 4R Principle
A widely used nutrient-management framework is:
Right Source+Right Rate+Right Time+Right Place  +   +   +    

Right source
Choose an appropriate N fertilizer/source.

Right rate
Apply enough but avoid unnecessary excess.

Right time
Synchronize N availability with crop demand.

Right place
Position N where roots can access it while reducing losses.

69. Nitrogen Application Timing
Nitrogen demand changes during crop development.
General pattern:
Early Growth→Vegetative Demand↑→Reproductive Demand→Grain/Fruit/Seed Filling 
Exact timing differs greatly among crops.
Therefore a universal “one-time N application” is not appropriate for all crops.

70. Split Nitrogen Application
Instead of applying the entire N dose at once, N may be divided into several applications.
Advantages can include:
- better synchronization with crop demand,
- reduced losses,
- improved recovery,
- flexibility under variable rainfall/irrigation.
Conceptually:
Large Single Dose→Loss Risk↑ 
while:

Well-timed Split Applications→N Synchronization↑ timed\ Split\ Applications
 N\ Synchronization

71. Nitrogen Placement
Possible methods include:
- broadcasting,
- band placement,
- side-dressing,
- fertigation,
- deep placement in specific systems,
- incorporation.
The objective is:

72. Nitrogen and Irrigation
Nitrogen and water management should be coordinated.
Too much irrigation after nitrate application can increase:
NO3−→Leaching 
Too little water can restrict:
- nutrient transport,
- root activity,
- N uptake.
Therefore:
N Management+Water Management 
should be considered together.

73. Nitrogen and Soil Organic Matter
Organic matter is a major nitrogen reservoir.
Organic N↔Mineral N 
Microorganisms regulate this conversion.
Organic matter therefore provides a slow and dynamic source of nitrogen rather than behaving exactly
like soluble fertilizer.

74. Organic Manures and Nitrogen
Examples:
- farmyard manure,
- compost,
- poultry manure,
- green manure,
- crop residues.
Their N availability depends on:
- C:N ratio,
- decomposition,
- temperature,
- moisture,
- microbial activity,
- maturity.
Total N content does not equal immediately plant-available N.

75. Green Manuring
Green manure crops are incorporated into soil to add organic matter and nutrients.
Leguminous green manures can contribute biologically fixed N.
General pathway:

Legume→N Fixation→Plant Biomass→Soil Organic N→Mineralization 

76. Nitrogen from Crop Residues
Residues contain nitrogen.
But availability depends strongly on their C:N ratio.

Low/moderate C:N
Faster N release is more likely.

High C:N
High C:N→Immobilization Tendency 
Thus residue incorporation can temporarily reduce mineral N availability.

77. Nitrogen and Crop Rotation
Including legumes in rotation can contribute biological nitrogen fixation.
Example:
Cereal→Legume→Cereal 
Potential benefits:
- N cycling,
- diversification,
- soil biological activity,
- improved nutrient management.
However, the amount of N actually available to the following crop depends on:
- fixation,
- biomass production,
- residue retention,
- N export in harvested product,
- decomposition,
- timing.

78. Nitrogen and Cover Crops
Cover crops can:
- capture residual nitrate,
- reduce nitrate leaching,
- add biomass,
- contribute organic N,
- improve soil cover.
But cover crops can also temporarily immobilize N depending on species and residue quality.

79. Nitrogen and Soil Microorganisms
Microorganisms control major N transformations.
Important processes:
Mineralization  Nitrification 
Denitrification  Immobilization 
Therefore:
Soil Biology→N Cycling→N Availability 

80. Nitrogen and Soil pH
Soil pH affects:
- microbial activity,
- nitrification,
- nutrient availability,
- root growth,
- fertilizer response.
Extremely acidic or alkaline soils can reduce efficient N use.

81. Nitrogen and Waterlogging
Waterlogging reduces oxygen availability.
This can:
- reduce root respiration,
- reduce root activity,
- alter N transformations,
- increase denitrification,
- reduce nutrient uptake.
Waterlogging→O2↓→Denitrification Risk↑ 

82. Nitrogen and Drought
Drought can reduce:
- root activity,
- nutrient transport,
- microbial mineralization,
- N uptake.
Therefore:
Drought→N Uptake↓ 
Even if soil contains sufficient total nitrogen.

83. Nitrogen and Salinity
Salinity can interfere with:
- root water uptake,
- nutrient uptake,
- ion balance,
- plant metabolism.
Therefore:

Salinity→N Uptake/Assimilation Disturbance 
Balanced nutrition becomes particularly important under saline conditions.

84. Nitrogen and Temperature
Temperature influences:
- microbial decomposition,
- mineralization,
- nitrification,
- root activity,
- plant metabolism.
Therefore N availability is dynamic rather than fixed.

85. Nitrogen Diagnostic Framework
When a crop appears nitrogen deficient, do not immediately assume fertilizer is the answer.
Consider:
N Supply+Root Access+Water+pH+Aeration+Other Nutrients+Crop Demand

89. Nitrogen Management in Cereals
Nitrogen commonly influences:
- tillering,
- leaf area,
- grain number,
- grain filling,
- protein concentration.
A generalized pathway:
N→Tillers→Canopy→Photosynthesis→Grain Formation/Filling 
Excess N can increase:
- lodging,
- delayed maturity,
- disease risk,
- N losses.

90. Nitrogen in Pulses
Legumes can obtain substantial N through biological fixation when effective symbiosis occurs.
Therefore fertilizer N requirements may differ from cereals.
However, legumes are not automatically independent of soil N.
Early growth may depend on soil N before fixation becomes fully effective.

91. Nitrogen in Oilseed Crops
Nitrogen affects:
- vegetative growth,
- reproductive development,
- seed formation,
- protein content.
But excessive N can alter the balance between vegetative growth and reproductive/yield outcomes and
may affect oil/protein composition depending on crop.

92. Nitrogen in Vegetables
Nitrogen strongly influences:
- leaf area,
- shoot growth,

What is nitrogen and why is it important?
Nitrogen is an essential primary macronutrient required by plants in relatively large
amounts. It is a constituent of amino acids, proteins, enzymes, chlorophyll, nucleic

Phosphorus (P) is an essential primary macronutrient required by plants for energy transfer, nucleic

Plants mainly absorb phosphorus from the soil solution as:

22. Phosphorus Uptake and Soil pH
The dominant phosphate species changes with pH.

- near neutral to alkaline conditions → increasing HPO42−HPO 4  2- .

Plants can absorb both forms.

23. Why Phosphorus Is Often Poorly Available
A major characteristic of soil P is its low mobility and strong fixation/retention in many soils.
Therefore:
Total Soil P≠Plant Available P 
A soil can contain considerable total P while having relatively low solution P available to plants.

24. Phosphorus Fixation
Phosphorus fixation refers broadly to processes that convert soluble or readily available phosphate into

Two major situations are:

Acid soils
Phosphate can interact strongly with:
- iron oxides,
- aluminum oxides,
- iron/aluminum minerals.

Calcareous/alkaline soils
Phosphate can precipitate or become associated with:
- calcium,
- calcium carbonates,
- Ca-phosphate minerals.
Thus:
Available P↓when P becomes strongly retained or precipitated   

25. Phosphorus in Acid Soils
In strongly acidic soils:
P+Fe/Al→Strongly Retained P 
This can reduce P availability.

Liming an acidic soil to an appropriate pH can sometimes improve P availability by reducing extreme
acidity and changing the chemistry of Fe/Al-associated P.

26. Phosphorus in Alkaline/Calcareous Soils
In calcareous soils, phosphorus can interact with calcium.
Conceptually:
P+Ca→Less Soluble Ca−P Compounds 
Therefore high soil pH does not automatically mean high P availability.

27. Phosphorus Availability Curve
Phosphorus availability is often greatest in a moderately acidic to near-neutral pH range, although the
exact optimum depends on:
- soil mineralogy,
- organic matter,
- calcium carbonate,
- Fe/Al oxides,
- crop,
- extraction method.

Extreme pH→P Fixation/Precipitation↑→P Availability↓

Phosphorus is generally much less mobile than nitrate.

2. root interception,

29. Phosphorus Movement by Diffusion
Diffusion is often the most important mechanism supplying P to roots.
High P concentration→Low P concentration 
Phosphate moves through the soil solution toward the root surface as roots absorb P.

30. Phosphorus and Soil Moisture
Adequate soil moisture is important for P movement by diffusion.
Under drought:
Soil Moisture↓→P Diffusion↓→P Uptake↓

- changes in lateral root growth,
- altered root/shoot allocation,
- increased root exploration.

- soil P,
- fungal species,
- soil conditions,

3. Potassium (K)
## 1. Introduction
Potassium (K) is an essential primary macronutrient required for normal plant growth, water
relations, stomatal regulation, enzyme activation, photosynthesis, carbohydrate transport, protein
metabolism, stress tolerance, and crop quality.
The three primary macronutrients are:
N−P−K 
A useful way to remember them is:
- Nitrogen (N) → growth, proteins, chlorophyll
- Phosphorus (P) → energy, DNA/RNA, roots, reproduction
- Potassium (K) → water regulation, enzymes, transport, strength and stress tolerance
The central concept of potassium is:
K→Osmotic Regulation+Enzyme Activation+Stomatal Control+Transport+Stress Tolerance 
Unlike nitrogen and phosphorus, potassium is not incorporated into major organic structural
molecules in the same way. It primarily functions as a soluble inorganic ion:
K+ 

2. Definition of Potassium
Potassium is an essential primary macronutrient absorbed by plants mainly as
K+K +, functioning primarily in osmotic regulation, stomatal movement, enzyme
activation, ion balance, photosynthesis, carbohydrate transport, protein metabolism
and resistance to environmental stresses.

Exam-ready definition
Potassium is an essential plant macronutrient that is absorbed mainly as the K+K +
ion and plays major roles in water relations, stomatal regulation, enzyme activation,
ionic balance, photosynthesis, assimilate transport, stress tolerance and crop quality.

3. Why Potassium Is a Macronutrient
Plants require potassium in relatively large quantities.

Primary macronutrients
N, P, K 

Secondary macronutrients
Ca, Mg, S 
Potassium is often present in plants in concentrations comparable to or greater than many other mineral
nutrients.

Plants primarily absorb potassium as:

6. Potassium and Osmotic Regulation
One of the most important functions of K is regulation of osmotic potential.
Potassium ions contribute to the concentration of solutes inside cells.
This helps cells:
- retain water,
- maintain turgor,
- expand,
- regulate cellular water status.
Conceptually:
K+↑→Solute concentration↑→Osmotic adjustment→Water retention/turgor 

7. Potassium and Turgor
Turgor pressure is the pressure generated when water enters a plant cell and presses the plasma
membrane against the cell wall.
Turgor is important for:
- cell expansion,
- leaf rigidity,
- stomatal movement,
- shoot growth,
- maintenance of plant structure.
Potassium contributes to maintaining appropriate cellular osmotic conditions.

8. Potassium and Stomatal Regulation
This is one of the most important functions of K.
Stomata are pores mainly located on leaves.
They control:
- CO₂ entry,
- water vapor loss,
- transpiration.
Guard cells regulate stomatal opening partly through ion movement.

9. Potassium and Transpiration
Stomata regulate water vapor loss.
Therefore K indirectly influences transpiration.
K→Stomatal Regulation→Transpiration Control 
Adequate K can improve the plant's ability to regulate water loss, especially under changing
environmental conditions.

10. Potassium and Water-Use Efficiency
Potassium can contribute to water-use efficiency by helping coordinate:
- stomatal conductance,
- CO₂ uptake,
- photosynthesis,
- transpiration,
- cellular water status.
Conceptually:
K Adequacy→Better Stomatal Regulation→Balanced CO2 Uptake+Water Loss 
However, high K does not automatically guarantee high water-use efficiency.

11. Potassium and Enzyme Activation
Potassium is an important activator or regulator of many enzymes.
It can influence:
- enzyme conformation,
- enzyme activity,
- metabolic reactions,
- carbohydrate metabolism,
- protein synthesis.
Thus:

K+→Enzyme Activation→Metabolism 
Hundreds of enzyme systems are influenced directly or indirectly by K-dependent processes.

12. Potassium and Photosynthesis
Potassium supports photosynthesis through several mechanisms.
It helps regulate:
- stomatal opening,
- CO₂ entry,
- enzyme activity,
- chloroplast function,
- carbohydrate metabolism.
The relationship can be represented as:
K→Stomatal Control→CO2 Entry→Photosynthesis 
Therefore K deficiency can reduce photosynthetic efficiency.

35. Potassium in Soil
Soil potassium can be divided conceptually into:
Solution K↔Exchangeable K↔Non-exchangeable K↔Mineral K exchangeable\ K  Mineral\ K  
These pools are dynamic.

36. Solution Potassium
This is K dissolved in the soil solution.
It is directly available for root uptake.
But the quantity in solution at any particular time is relatively small compared with the total amount of
K in soil minerals.

37. Exchangeable Potassium
Exchangeable K is held on negatively charged soil surfaces and can be exchanged with other cations.
Exchangeable K↔Solution K 
This pool is an important source of plant-available K.

38. Non-Exchangeable Potassium
Some potassium is held more strongly within certain soil minerals.

It may become available over time through:
- mineral weathering,
- changes in soil chemistry,
- wetting-drying,
- biological processes.

39. Mineral Potassium
A very large fraction of total soil K may occur in minerals such as:
- feldspars,
- micas.
However:
Total Soil K≠Immediately Available K 
Most mineral K is not immediately accessible to crops.

40. Potassium Fixation
Some soils can temporarily trap K between layers of certain clay minerals.
This process is commonly called K fixation.
It can occur particularly in soils containing certain 2:1 clay minerals.
Conceptually:
Exchangeable K→Fixed/Non−exchangeable K 
This can reduce short-term availability.

41. Potassium Release
Fixed or mineral K can gradually return to available pools:
Mineral/Fixed K→Exchangeable K→Solution K→Plant 
This creates a dynamic equilibrium.

42. Potassium Leaching
Potassium is generally more mobile than phosphorus but less consistently mobile than nitrate.
Leaching can be significant in:
- sandy soils,
- low-CEC soils,
- high-rainfall conditions,
- excessive irrigation.
Conceptually:
High Rainfall/Irrigation+Low CEC→K Leaching Risk↑ 

43. Soil CEC and Potassium
CEC is important because K⁺ is a cation.
Soils with higher CEC can generally retain more exchangeable K.
CEC↑→Cation Retention Capacity↑ 
But high CEC does not automatically mean high available K.

44. Sandy vs Clay Soil and Potassium
Sandy soil
Often:
- low CEC,
- lower K retention,
- greater leaching risk.

Clay-rich soil
Often:
- higher CEC,
- greater K retention.
But specific clay minerals can also fix K strongly.

Therefore:
K Availability=f(CEC+Clay Mineralogy+Moisture+Crop+Management)

Adequate soil moisture is necessary for nutrient transport and root activity.
Under severe drought:
Soil Moisture↓→Root Activity↓→K Uptake↓ 
Thus K deficiency can sometimes be aggravated by drought even when soil K is not extremely low.

Potassium is highly mobile within plants.

Older/lower leaves  
because K can be redistributed toward younger actively growing tissues.

At the same time, physiological stress can become especially visible at leaf margins and tips.
Thus:

This is a useful diagnostic pattern but is not sufficient for diagnosis by itself.

True direct K toxicity is less commonly observed than deficiency, but excessive K application can

K also interacts with calcium nutrition.

Excessive K can sometimes interfere with Ca uptake or balance depending on crop and soil conditions.
Thus:

is more important than maximizing K alone.

55. Potassium × Nitrogen

57. Potassium and Saline Irrigation Water
Since the previous module topic was saline irrigation water, this relationship is particularly important.

- drainage,
- salt management,

Waterlogging reduces root-zone oxygen.

- cation exchange,
- nutrient interactions,
- K availability.

- microbial activity,
- soil structure,
- water retention,
- residue decomposition.

A simplified cycle is:

## 1. Introduction
Calcium (Ca) is an essential secondary macronutrient required for plant growth, cell-wall formation,
membrane stability, cell division, root development, signaling, structural integrity, and regulation of

The three secondary macronutrients are:

Unlike nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium, calcium is especially important in structural stability and
newly developing tissues.

Calcium is an essential secondary macronutrient absorbed mainly as Ca2+Ca  2+ ,

14. Why Calcium Deficiency Appears in Young
Tissues
This is a very important diagnostic concept.
Calcium is generally poorly mobile in the phloem compared with nutrients such as N, P, K and Mg.
Therefore calcium cannot easily be redistributed from older leaves to developing tissues when the new
tissues need it.
Consequently:
Ca Deficiency→Young Leaves+Growing Points→Symptoms 
This is almost the opposite of potassium deficiency, which often appears first in older leaves.

In the soil
Calcium is a cation:

In the plant

Thus:
Ca movement within plant≈strongly dependent on xylem water flow 
This is extremely important for crop management.

45. Magnesium in Soil
Magnesium occurs in several soil pools.
A simplified model:
Solution Mg↔Exchangeable Mg↔Mineral Mg 

46. Soil Solution Magnesium
Magnesium dissolved in soil water occurs mainly as:
Mg2+  
This is the form directly accessible to plant roots.

47. Exchangeable Magnesium
Clay and organic matter have negatively charged exchange sites.
Mg²⁺ can be held on these sites:
Soil colloid−↔Mg2+   Mg  2+   
Exchangeable Mg serves as an important reservoir for the soil solution.

48. Total Magnesium vs Available Magnesium
A soil may contain large amounts of total Mg while plants still experience nutritional problems.
Therefore:
Total Mg≠Plant Available Mg 
Availability depends on:
- soil texture,
- CEC,
- pH,
- moisture,
- cation balance,
- root activity,
- salinity.

49. Magnesium Leaching
Magnesium can be leached from soils, especially under conditions such as:
- high rainfall,
- acidic soils,
- coarse-textured soils,
- low CEC.
Thus:
High Rainfall+Low Retention→Mg Leaching 

50. Magnesium and Sandy Soil
Sandy soils generally have:
- low CEC,
- lower nutrient retention,
- greater potential for leaching.
Therefore Mg deficiency can be more likely under intensive cropping and high rainfall/irrigation when
Mg inputs are inadequate.

51. Magnesium and Soil pH
Soil acidity can influence Mg availability.
Acidic, highly leached soils may have reduced exchangeable Mg.
Liming can sometimes increase Mg supply if a magnesium-containing amendment is used.

59. Magnesium and Potassium Imbalance

## 3. Boron (B)
### 3.1 Introduction
Boron is an essential micronutrient required particularly for:
- cell wall development,
- cell division,
- meristem growth,
- reproductive development,
- pollen germination,
- pollen tube growth,
- sugar transport,
- membrane function,
- carbohydrate metabolism,
- root and shoot development.
Although plants require only a small amount of boron, the difference between deficiency and toxicity
can be relatively narrow.
Therefore:
Boron must be supplied in the right amount  

### 3.2 Definition
Boron is an essential plant micronutrient that is particularly important for cell wall
formation and stability, cell division and expansion, meristem development,
reproductive growth, pollen germination, pollen tube growth, and membrane and
carbohydrate-related processes.

Exam-ready definition
Boron is an essential micronutrient involved mainly in cell wall structure,
meristematic growth, cell division, reproductive development, pollen germination and
pollen tube growth, and the maintenance of normal plant growth.

3.5 Boron and Cell Walls
One of the most important functions of boron is its association with cell-wall structure.
Boron forms complexes involving cell-wall components, particularly:

B→RG−II cross−linking→Cell wall stability

B deficiency→Meristem damage→Growing point death

3.8 Boron and Root Growth
Boron is essential for normal root-tip development.
Deficiency can result in:
- restricted root growth,
- thickened or abnormal root tips,
- reduced root elongation,
- poor root branching,

Therefore:
B→Root meristem→Root growth

- distorted new leaves,
- reduced shoot elongation,
- branching abnormalities,

Boron is particularly important for:
- flower development,
- pollen formation,
- pollen germination,
- pollen tube growth,
- fertilization,
- seed development.
Therefore:
B→Pollen→Fertilization→Seed Formation

Because boron is important for reproductive processes, deficiency can result in:

- malformed fruits,
- empty grains/seeds,

B deficiency→Pollen/Fertilization problems→Fruit/Seed set↓

B→Carbohydrate transport/metabolism 

3.15 Boron and Membranes
Boron contributes to normal membrane function and membrane-associated processes.
Deficiency can disturb:

- ion transport,
- cellular metabolism.

3.16 Boron and Hormones
Boron interacts with plant developmental processes and hormone-related signaling, including effects
associated with:
- auxin,
- cytokinins,
- ethylene.
However, boron should not be described simply as an "auxin nutrient"; its effects are broader and

5. COPPER (Cu)
### 5.1 Introduction
Copper is an essential micronutrient required in very small quantities. It is particularly important in:
- electron transport,
- photosynthesis,
- respiration,
- enzyme activity,
- lignin formation,
- antioxidant defense,
- reproductive development,
- pollen fertility,
- carbohydrate and protein metabolism.
Copper participates in many reactions because it can readily change oxidation state:
Cu+↔Cu2+Cu +  Cu  2+ 
This makes copper particularly valuable in electron-transfer reactions.

### 5.2 Definition
Copper is an essential plant micronutrient that functions mainly in electron-transfer
reactions, photosynthesis, respiration, enzyme activity, lignin formation, antioxidant
defense and reproductive development.

Exam-ready definition
Copper is an essential micronutrient involved in electron transport, photosynthesis,
respiration, enzyme activation, lignin synthesis, antioxidant protection and
reproductive development of plants.

5.3 Forms of Copper
Plants primarily absorb copper as:
Cu2+  
Copper can exist in different oxidation states:

Cu+↔Cu2+Cu +  Cu  2+ 
This redox behavior is central to its biological function.

5.4 Major Functions of Copper
Copper participates in:
1. electron transport,
2. photosynthesis,
3. respiration,
4. enzyme activity,
5. antioxidant defense,
6. lignin formation,
7. carbohydrate metabolism,
8. protein metabolism,
9. reproductive development,
10.pollen fertility,
11.stress responses.

5.5 Copper and Electron Transfer
The most fundamental chemical characteristic of Cu is its ability to participate in reversible redox
reactions:
Cu+↔Cu2+   
Therefore:
Cu→Electron transfer→Energy metabolism 

5.6 Copper and Photosynthesis
Copper is an important component of plastocyanin, a copper-containing protein involved in
photosynthetic electron transport.
Simplified pathway:

Cu→Plastocyanin→Electron transport→Photosynthesis 
Plastocyanin transfers electrons between components of the photosynthetic electron transport chain.

5.7 Copper and Photosynthetic Electron
Transport
Copper is particularly associated with electron movement between:
Cytochrome b6f→Plastocyanin→Photosystem I b 6f   Therefore Cu deficiency can reduce photosynthetic electron transport.

5.8 Copper and Respiration
Copper is also associated with mitochondrial respiration.
A major example is:
Cytochrome c oxidase  
This enzyme complex contains copper and participates in the mitochondrial electron transport chain.
Therefore:
Cu→Cytochrome c oxidase→Respiration→ATP production 

5.9 Copper and Energy Metabolism
Because Cu participates in both photosynthetic and respiratory electron transport:
Cu→Photosynthesis+Respiration→Energy metabolism 
This is why severe Cu deficiency can strongly affect overall plant vigor.

5.10 Copper as an Enzyme Cofactor
Copper is associated with several important enzymes and proteins.
Examples include:

- plastocyanin,
- cytochrome c oxidase,
- Cu/Zn-superoxide dismutase,
- polyphenol oxidases,
- laccases,
- amine oxidases.
Thus:
Cu→Enzyme/protein function→Metabolism 

5.11 Copper and Antioxidant Defense
Copper is a component of:
Cu/Zn-Superoxide Dismutase  
This enzyme helps detoxify superoxide radicals.
Simplified:
2O2−+2H+→H2O2+O22O 2 -+2H +  H 2O 2+O 2
Therefore:
Cu→Cu/Zn−SOD→ROS protection 

5.12 Copper and Reactive Oxygen Species
Under stress, plants can accumulate reactive oxygen species (ROS).
Adequate copper contributes to antioxidant defense.
Therefore:
Cu deficiency→Antioxidant capacity↓→Oxidative stress risk↑ 

5.13 Copper and Lignin Formation
Copper is important in enzymes involved in lignification, including laccases.
Lignin provides:
- structural strength,

- vascular support,
- mechanical rigidity,
- barriers against some pathogens.
Therefore:
Cu→Lignification→Cell/tissue strength 

5.14 Copper and Stem Strength
Reduced lignification under severe Cu deficiency can result in:
- weak stems,
- bending,
- lodging,
- poor structural integrity.
Thus:
Cu deficiency→Lignification↓→Structural strength↓Cu\ deficiency  Lignification  Structural\ strength 5.15 Copper and Reproductive Development
Copper is particularly important for reproductive development.
It contributes to:
- flower development,
- pollen formation,
- pollen viability,
- pollen fertility,
- seed formation.
Therefore:
Cu→Pollen fertility→Fertilization→Seed formation 

5.16 Copper Deficiency and Flowering
Severe Cu deficiency can result in:
- delayed flowering,
- poor flowering,
- reduced pollen viability,
- poor seed formation,
- reduced fertility.
Therefore reproductive problems can be an important diagnostic clue.

5.17 Copper and Carbohydrate Metabolism
Copper participates in enzymes involved in carbohydrate metabolism and distribution.
Severe deficiency can therefore reduce:
- photosynthetic performance,
- carbohydrate production,
- carbohydrate utilization,
- growth.

5.18 Copper and Protein Metabolism
Copper-dependent enzymes participate in metabolic reactions that influence nitrogen and protein
metabolism.
Thus Cu deficiency can indirectly affect:
N metabolism→Protein synthesis→Growth 

5.19 Copper Deficiency Symptoms
Typical symptoms include:
- young leaves becoming pale,
- leaf curling,
- leaf-tip dieback,

- reduced shoot growth,
- weak stems,
- delayed flowering,
- poor pollen fertility,
- reduced seed formation,
- reduced tillering in some cereals,
- chlorosis,
- necrotic leaf tips under severe deficiency.
Symptoms vary significantly among crops.

5.20 Copper Deficiency and Young Tissues
Copper is relatively immobile in the phloem in many plants.
Therefore deficiency often appears in:
- young leaves,
- shoot tips,
- reproductive tissues.
A simplified pattern is:
Cu deficiency→Young tissue symptoms 

5.21 Copper Deficiency in Cereals
Possible symptoms include:
- leaf-tip chlorosis,
- leaf curling,
- poor tillering,
- delayed maturity,
- weak stems,
- poor grain formation.
Copper deficiency can be especially important in some:
- wheat,

- barley,
- oats,
- rice.

5.22 Copper Deficiency and Reproduction
One of the important consequences is:
Cu deficiency→Pollen fertility↓→Seed set↓ 
Therefore a crop may have reasonable vegetative growth but still experience reproductive problems
under severe deficiency.

5.23 Copper in Soil
Soil copper exists in several pools:
- soil solution,
- exchangeable/adsorbed Cu,
- organic matter-bound Cu,
- mineral-associated Cu.
Only a fraction is readily available to plants.
Therefore:
Total soil Cu≠Plant available Cu 

5.24 Soil pH and Copper
Generally:
pH↑→Cu adsorption/immobilization↑→Cu availability↓ 
Therefore Cu deficiency is more likely in some:
- high-pH soils,
- calcareous soils,
- highly organic soils.

5.25 Copper and Organic Matter
Copper has a strong affinity for organic matter.
Therefore:
Organic matter↑→Cu complexation/binding↑ 
This can reduce the immediately available Cu fraction in some soils.
This is particularly relevant in:
- peat-rich soils,
- high-organic-matter soils,
- soils receiving large organic inputs.

5.26 Copper and Sandy Soils
Coarse-textured soils can have low copper reserves and may experience Cu deficiency under certain
conditions.
Factors include:
- low organic matter,
- low CEC,
- leaching,
- low total Cu.

5.27 Copper and Soil Moisture
Soil moisture affects:
- root activity,
- diffusion,
- redox conditions,
- microbial processes.
Both drought and excessive water can therefore alter Cu availability and uptake.

5.28 Copper Toxicity
Copper is required in very small quantities and becomes toxic at elevated concentrations.
Excess Cu can cause:
- root growth inhibition,
- reduced root elongation,
- chlorosis,
- oxidative stress,
- impaired nutrient uptake,
- reduced shoot growth.
A major toxicity effect is often observed in roots.
Cu excess→Root toxicity→Water/Nutrient uptake↓ 

5.29 Sources of Copper
Common agricultural sources include:
- copper sulfate,
- copper chelates,
- copper oxide,
- copper-containing micronutrient mixtures,
- some organic amendments.
A common fertilizer source is:
CuSO4 

5.30 Copper Application
Copper can be applied through:
- soil application,
- foliar application,
- fertigation,
- seed treatment in some systems,

- micronutrient mixtures.
The correct method depends on:
- crop,
- soil test,
- deficiency severity,
- formulation,
- local recommendations.

5.31 Copper and Disease Management
Copper compounds are also used in agriculture as fungicidal/bactericidal products, but this is
different from copper nutrition.
Therefore:
Copper fertilizer≠Copper fungicide 
The biological role of Cu as a micronutrient should not be confused with the external use of coppercontaining crop-protection products.

5.32 Copper — Master Pathway
Cu→Electron Transfer→Photosynthesis+Respiration 
and:
Cu→Enzymes+Antioxidant Defense 
and:
Cu→Lignin+Reproductive Development 
Therefore:
Cu=Electron Transfer + Enzymes + Structure + Reproduction

respiration, Cu/Zn-SOD, lignin, pollen and seed formation.

6. MOLYBDENUM (Mo)
### 6.1 Introduction
Molybdenum is an essential micronutrient required in extremely small quantities.
It is fundamentally different from many micronutrients because its most important functions are closely
associated with:
- nitrogen metabolism,
- nitrate reduction,
- biological nitrogen fixation,
- sulfur metabolism,
- enzyme activity.
The two most important Mo-dependent processes to remember are:
Nitrate Reduction  
and:
Nitrogen Fixation  

### 6.2 Definition
Molybdenum is an essential plant micronutrient that functions as a cofactor of
enzymes involved particularly in nitrate assimilation and biological nitrogen fixation,
thereby playing a critical role in plant nitrogen metabolism.

Exam-ready definition
Molybdenum is an essential micronutrient required for nitrate reductase and
nitrogenase activity and is therefore essential for nitrate assimilation and biological
nitrogen fixation.

6.3 Forms of Molybdenum
Plants generally absorb molybdenum primarily as:
MoO42−  
which is the molybdate ion.

6.4 Major Functions of Molybdenum
Molybdenum is involved in:
1. nitrate reduction,
2. nitrogen assimilation,
3. biological nitrogen fixation,
4. nitrogenase activity,
5. nitrate reductase activity,
6. sulfur metabolism,
7. aldehyde oxidase activity,
8. stress/developmental processes through Mo-dependent enzymes.

6.5 Molybdenum and Nitrogen Metabolism
The simplest memory is:
Mo→N metabolism 
Without adequate Mo, plants cannot efficiently perform certain essential nitrogen transformations.

6.6 Molybdenum and Nitrate Reductase
Plants often absorb nitrogen as nitrate:
NO3−NO 3 Before nitrate can be incorporated efficiently into organic nitrogen compounds, it must undergo
reduction.
The first major step is:
NO3−→NO2−NO 3 -  NO 2 This reaction is catalyzed by:
Nitrate reductase  
Molybdenum is a key component of the nitrate reductase cofactor system.
Therefore:
Mo→Nitrate reductase→NO3−→NO2− 

6.7 Nitrate Assimilation Pathway
The broader pathway is:
NO3−→NO2−→NH4+→Amino acids→Proteins 
Molybdenum is particularly critical in the first reduction step.

6.8 Why Mo Deficiency Can Look Like Nitrogen
Deficiency
Because Mo is required for nitrate utilization:
Mo↓→Nitrate reduction↓→N assimilation↓Mo  Nitrate\ reduction  N\ assimilation Therefore plants may show symptoms resembling nitrogen deficiency even when nitrate is present in
the soil.
This is a very important diagnostic concept.
Mo deficiency can produce a functional N deficiency 

6.9 Molybdenum and Nitrogen Fixation
Biological nitrogen fixation converts atmospheric nitrogen into biologically usable nitrogen.
The overall reaction can be represented approximately as:
N2→NH3/NH4+ 
The enzyme responsible is:
Nitrogenase  
Nitrogenase contains a Mo-containing catalytic component in the most common Mo-dependent
nitrogenase system.

6.10 Molybdenum in Legumes
Legumes can form symbiotic relationships with nitrogen-fixing bacteria such as:
Rhizobium/related rhizobia /  

The pathway is:
Legume→Root nodule→Rhizobia→Nitrogenase→N2 fixation 
Molybdenum is therefore particularly important for effective biological nitrogen fixation.

6.11 Molybdenum and Nitrogenase
Nitrogenase is a complex enzyme system.
The most common Mo-dependent nitrogenase contains:
- Fe protein,
- MoFe protein.
The MoFe protein contains the important:
FeMo-cofactor  
The FeMo-cofactor is central to nitrogen reduction.
Thus:
Mo→FeMo-cofactor→Nitrogenase→N2 fixation cofactor
 Nitrogenase  N 2\ fixation  

6.12 Biological Nitrogen Fixation
A simplified overall reaction is:
N2+8H++8e−+16ATP→2NH3+H2+16ADP+16PiN 2+8H ++8e -+16ATP  2NH 3+H 2+16ADP+16P i
The actual nitrogenase system is more complex, but this equation illustrates the high energy
requirement.
Molybdenum is a critical component of the conventional nitrogenase system.

6.13 Mo and Legume Nodules
Adequate Mo supports effective nodule function.
Mo deficiency can therefore lead to:
- poor nitrogen fixation,
- reduced nodule effectiveness,

- reduced plant N status,
- poor growth.
Thus:
Mo deficiency→Nitrogenase activity↓→N2 fixation↓→Plant N status↓ 

6.14 Molybdenum and Non-Legumes
Mo is not only important for legumes.
Non-leguminous crops also require Mo because they use nitrate reduction.
Thus:
Mo→Nitrate assimilation 
is relevant to many crops.

6.15 Molybdenum and Sulfur Metabolism
Mo is also involved in enzymes such as:
- sulfite oxidase,
- xanthine dehydrogenase,
- aldehyde oxidase,
- xanthine oxidoreductase.
Thus Mo has functions beyond nitrogen metabolism.

6.16 Molybdenum and Sulfite Oxidase
Sulfite oxidase participates in sulfur metabolism.
It converts sulfite toward sulfate-related metabolism.
Therefore:
Mo→Sulfite oxidase→Sulfur metabolism 

6.17 Molybdenum and Aldehyde Oxidase
Aldehyde oxidase is involved in pathways related to:
- hormone metabolism,
- particularly ABA and auxin-related processes,
- aldehyde metabolism.
Therefore Mo can indirectly influence plant development and stress responses.

6.18 Molybdenum and Hormones
Mo-dependent aldehyde oxidases participate in the metabolism involved in production of certain plant
hormones/signaling molecules.
For example:
Mo→Aldehyde oxidase→ABA/auxin−related metabolism 
This is an advanced role and is less important for basic examination than nitrate reductase and
nitrogenase.

6.19 Molybdenum Deficiency Symptoms
Typical symptoms include:
- nitrogen-deficiency-like chlorosis,
- poor growth,
- reduced nitrate assimilation,
- reduced nitrogen fixation,
- poor nodulation/function in legumes,
- leaf curling,
- marginal scorching/necrosis in some crops,
- reduced reproductive growth in severe cases.

6.20 Molybdenum and Older Leaves
Mo is not as strongly phloem-mobile as N, but Mo deficiency symptoms can occur in older/mature
leaves because nitrogen metabolism and redistribution are affected.
The exact symptom pattern is crop-dependent.
Therefore leaf age alone should not be used for diagnosis.

6.21 Molybdenum and Cauliflower
A classic example is whiptail of cauliflower.
Symptoms include:
- narrow,
- elongated,
- distorted leaves,
- reduced leaf blade development.
This is strongly associated with Mo deficiency.
Mo deficiency in cauliflower→Whiptail 

6.22 Molybdenum and Legumes
In legumes, deficiency may result in:
- poor nitrogen fixation,
- poor plant vigor,
- reduced leaf nitrogen,
- reduced protein formation,
- reduced yield.
The key pathway is:
Mo→Nitrogenase→N2 fixation 

6.23 Molybdenum and Soil pH
This is one of the most important soil relationships.
Unlike Fe, Zn, Mn and Cu, molybdenum availability generally increases as soil pH increases.

Conceptually:
pH↑→Mo availability↑ 
Therefore Mo deficiency is more likely in:
- acidic soils,
- strongly weathered acidic soils,
- soils with strong Mo adsorption.

6.24 Liming and Molybdenum
Liming acidic soils can increase Mo availability.
Therefore:
Liming→pH↑→Mo availability↑ 
This is the opposite trend to several other micronutrients.
For example:
pH↑→Fe/Zn/Mn/Cu availability↓ 
while:
pH↑→Mo availability↑ 
This is an extremely useful exam point.

6.25 Molybdenum and Soil Adsorption
Molybdate can be strongly adsorbed by certain soil minerals, particularly under acidic conditions.
As pH increases:
Mo adsorption↓→Mo in soil solution↑Mo\ adsorption  Mo\ in\ soil\
solution Therefore availability improves.

6.26 Molybdenum and Sandy Soils
Sandy soils may have relatively low nutrient reserves and can lose molybdate through leaching under
some conditions.

However, soil pH and mineralogy remain major controls.

6.27 Molybdenum and Soil Organic Matter
Organic matter can influence Mo availability through:
- adsorption,
- complexation,
- microbial cycling.
The exact effect depends on soil characteristics.

6.28 Molybdenum Toxicity
Molybdenum toxicity is less common in crop production than deficiency.
Excess Mo can:
- disrupt plant metabolism,
- accumulate in plants,
- create livestock-related problems when forage contains excessive Mo.
This is important because high-Mo forage can contribute to molybdenosis in grazing animals through
interference with copper metabolism.
Thus:
Excess Mo in forage→Cu antagonism→Animal health problems 
This is an important agriculture-specific point.

6.29 Sources of Molybdenum
Common Mo fertilizer sources include:
- sodium molybdate,
- ammonium molybdate,
- molybdenum-containing seed treatments,
- Mo-enriched micronutrient mixtures.
Examples:

Na2MoO4  (NH4)2MoO4 

6.30 Molybdenum Application
Mo can be supplied through:
- soil application,
- foliar application,
- seed treatment,
- seed coating,
- fertigation.
For legumes, seed treatment or inoculation-related approaches may be useful where appropriate.
Actual rates should be based on crop, soil and local recommendations because the required amount is
extremely small.

6.31 Molybdenum and Seed Treatment
Because plants need only very small amounts of Mo, seed treatment can be an efficient delivery
method in some systems.
This is particularly relevant to legumes where Mo supports nitrogen fixation.

6.32 Molybdenum and Rhizobium
It is important to distinguish:
Mo does not replace Rhizobium 
Instead:
Rhizobium+Suitable host+Mo+favorable conditions→Effective nitrogen fixation 
Mo supports the nitrogenase system; it does not itself perform biological nitrogen fixation.

7. CHLORINE (Cl)
### 7.1 Introduction
Chlorine is an essential plant micronutrient required in very small amounts.
It is mainly absorbed as:
Cl− 
Chloride has important functions in:
- osmotic regulation,
- cell hydration,
- stomatal regulation,
- electrical/charge balance,
- photosynthesis,
- water splitting in Photosystem II,
- nutrient transport,
- enzyme regulation,
- disease and stress responses.
An important point is that chlorine is required in much smaller amounts than sodium chloride used
as a salt, so:
Essential Cl≠Excess salt    

### 7.2 Definition
Chlorine is an essential plant micronutrient absorbed mainly as chloride (Cl−Cl -)
and involved in osmotic regulation, charge balance, stomatal function, photosynthesis,
water oxidation in Photosystem II, and overall cellular water and ion balance.

Chlorine is an essential micronutrient required mainly as chloride ion for osmotic

- growth.
Chloride contributes to the ion balance needed to maintain this turgor.

8. NICKEL (Ni)
### 8.1 Introduction
Nickel is an essential plant micronutrient required in extremely small quantities.
It is best remembered for one major function:
Nickel = Urease  
Nickel is required for the proper functioning of urease, an enzyme that breaks down urea-derived
compounds.
Nickel is also involved in:
- nitrogen metabolism,
- urea metabolism,
- seed viability,
- iron metabolism,
- antioxidant processes,
- certain enzyme systems.

### 8.2 Definition
Nickel is an essential plant micronutrient required primarily as a component of the
metalloenzyme urease and is therefore important for urea metabolism and nitrogen
utilization.

Exam-ready definition
Nickel is an essential micronutrient required for urease activity and therefore plays an
important role in urea metabolism, nitrogen utilization, seed development and overall
plant metabolic function.

Ni→Urease→Urea metabolism

A micronutrient is called "micro" because plants generally require it in much smaller quantities than
