## Module 5 — Soil Testing & Diagnosis
1. Why Soil Testing Is Required
## 1. Introduction
Soil testing is one of the most important tools for understanding the chemical, physical, and
sometimes biological condition of soil before making management decisions.
A farmer may look at a field and see that the soil is:
- dark,
- dry,
- wet,
- sandy,
- clayey,
- compacted,
- or apparently healthy,
but visual observation alone cannot reliably determine the amount of available nutrients, soil acidity,
salinity, sodicity, or other important properties.
Soil testing converts the hidden properties of soil into measurable information.
The basic idea is:
Soil Sample→Laboratory/Field Analysis→Soil Status→Diagnosis→Management Decision       

2. Definition of Soil Testing
Soil testing is the systematic collection and analysis of a representative soil sample to
determine selected physical, chemical, and/or biological properties of soil and to use
the results for crop, nutrient, irrigation, and soil-management decisions.
A soil test does not simply answer:
"Is this soil good or bad?"
Instead, it provides information such as:

- What is the soil pH?
- How much soluble salt is present?
- What is the organic carbon level?
- How much plant-available nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium is present?
- What is the CEC?
- Is sodium a problem?
- Is the soil physically restrictive?
- Is a nutrient deficiency likely?
- What management is appropriate?

3. Why Is Soil Testing Required?
The fundamental reason is:
You cannot reliably manage what you have not measured.   
Soil testing helps determine the actual condition of the root zone rather than relying only on
assumptions.

4. Major Objectives of Soil Testing
Soil testing is required for several major purposes:
1. determining nutrient status
2. identifying nutrient deficiencies
3. recommending fertilizers
4. avoiding excessive fertilizer application
5. determining soil acidity/alkalinity
6. identifying salinity problems
7. diagnosing sodicity-related problems
8. evaluating organic carbon
9. assessing soil fertility

10.monitoring changes in soil over time
11.improving fertilizer-use efficiency
12.supporting crop selection
13.improving irrigation and soil management
14.diagnosing poor crop growth
15.protecting soil and environmental quality.

5. Soil Testing and Nutrient Management
One of the most important applications is fertilizer management.
The simplified relationship is:
Soil Test→Nutrient Status→Crop Requirement→Fertilizer Recommendation 
    
Without soil testing, fertilizer application may be based mainly on:
- previous experience,
- generalized recommendations,
- neighboring farms,
- visual appearance,
- expected yield.
These approaches may not reflect the actual nutrient status of a particular field.

6. Soil Testing Helps Determine Available
Nutrients
The total amount of an element in soil is not necessarily available to plants.
For example:
Total Nutrient≠Plant-Available Nutrient   
A soil may contain considerable total phosphorus, but much of it may be in forms that roots cannot
readily access.

Similarly, total nitrogen does not directly indicate how much nitrogen will be available to a crop during
its growing period.
So soil testing attempts to measure defined operationally available or extractable nutrient pools.

12. Why pH Testing Is Important
Suppose a soil has adequate total iron.
That does not necessarily mean the crop can obtain enough iron.
At high pH, iron availability can become very low.
Thus:
Nutrient present≠nutrient available    
So testing pH helps interpret nutrient availability.

## Module 5 — Soil Testing & Diagnosis
2. Soil Sampling
## 1. Introduction
Soil sampling is the process of collecting a small but representative portion of soil from a field so that
it can be analyzed to determine its physical, chemical, and/or biological properties.
The laboratory may analyze only a few hundred grams of soil, but that small sample is expected to
represent a much larger field.
Therefore, the central principle is:
Representative sample→Reliable soil test→Correct diagnosis→Better management      
If the sample is poorly collected:
Poor sampling→Misleading test result→Wrong recommendation 
   
So soil sampling is often the most critical step in the entire soil-testing process.

2. Definition of Soil Sampling
Soil sampling is the systematic collection of representative soil portions from a defined
area and depth for laboratory or field analysis.
A good soil sample should represent:
- the correct field or management zone,
- the correct depth,
- the correct time,
- the relevant root zone,

- and the intended diagnostic purpose.

3. Why Soil Sampling Is Required
A laboratory cannot practically analyze the entire field.
Imagine a field containing thousands of tonnes of soil.
It is impossible to send all of it to a laboratory.
Instead:
Large field→small representative sample→laboratory     
The sample therefore acts as a statistical representation of the field.

4. Main Objectives of Soil Sampling
Soil sampling is carried out to:
1. determine nutrient status,
2. determine soil pH,
3. measure EC and assess salinity,
4. assess organic carbon,
5. evaluate physical properties,
6. diagnose poor crop growth,
7. determine fertilizer requirements,
8. evaluate soil fertility,
9. diagnose problem soils,
10.assess soil-water conditions,
11.monitor changes over time,
12.support precision agriculture,
13.evaluate reclamation needs,
14.assess root-zone conditions.

5. The Most Important Principle:
Representativeness
The most important rule is:
Sample should represent the area for which the result will be used   
For example, suppose a 10-hectare field has:
- sandy soil in one part,
- clayey soil in another,
- a low-lying wet area,
- and a saline patch.
Taking one sample from the middle and applying its result to the entire field may produce a misleading
diagnosis.
Instead, different management zones should be sampled separately.

6. Field Homogeneity
Before sampling, determine whether the field is reasonably uniform.
Consider differences in:
- soil color
- texture
- slope
- crop growth
- drainage
- irrigation
- previous crop
- fertilizer history
- manure application
- salinity
- erosion
- waterlogging.

A relatively uniform area can generally be treated as one sampling unit.
Different areas should generally be sampled separately.

7. Sampling Unit
A sampling unit is an area considered sufficiently uniform that one composite sample can reasonably
represent it for the particular purpose.
For example:
Field

├── Uniform Zone A → Sample A
├── Uniform Zone B → Sample B

A change of one pH unit represents approximately a 10-fold change in hydrogen-ion activity.
For example:
pH=5pH=5

pH=6pH=6

pH=7pH=7

ΔpH=1⇒10×change in H+ activity H +

Exam-ready definition
Molybdenum is an essential plant micronutrient required in extremely small
quantities, primarily for nitrogen metabolism. It forms part of the molybdenum

nitrogenase system responsible for biological nitrogen fixation. Plants commonly
acquire Mo as molybdate MoO42−MoO 4  2- . Unlike many micronutrients whose

availability decreases as soil pH increases, Mo availability generally increases with
increasing soil pH and is often low in acidic soils.
