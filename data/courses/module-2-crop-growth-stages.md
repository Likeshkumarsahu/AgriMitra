## Module 2 — Crop Growth Stages
1. Seed Stage
## 1. Introduction
The seed stage is the starting point of the life cycle of most seed-propagated crops. It begins with the
formation and maturation of the seed and continues until the seed begins the process of germination.
A seed is not simply a "small plant." It is a highly organized biological structure containing a
developing or mature embryo, stored food or nutrient reserves, and protective tissues that allow the
plant to survive unfavorable conditions and begin a new generation when environmental conditions
become suitable.
The basic sequence is:
Seed formation→Seed maturation→Seed dormancy/
storage→Imbibition→Germination       
Thus, the seed stage connects reproductive biology with the beginning of crop growth.

2. What Is a Seed?
A seed is a mature ovule that develops after fertilization in most flowering plants and contains the
structures necessary for producing a new plant.
The basic structure is:
Seed=Embryo+Storage tissue/reserves+Protective covering  =   +
  +    
Depending on the species, the storage tissue may be primarily:
- Endosperm
- Cotyledons
- Perisperm
- Or a combination of tissues
The seed protects the developing plant and provides nutrients during the early stages of germination.

3. Where Does the Seed Come From?
The reproductive sequence is:
Flower→Pollination→Fertilization→Ovule→Seed       
More precisely:
Ovule→fertilization + developmentSeed        
while:
Ovary→Fruit    
This distinction is extremely important.

Example
In a tomato:
- Ovule → seed
- Ovary → fruit
In wheat:
- Ovule → seed
- Ovary and associated tissues → caryopsis (grain)

4. Seed Stage in the Complete Crop Life Cycle
The seed stage is only one part of the complete crop lifecycle.
A generalized lifecycle is:
Seed→Germination→Seedling→Vegetative growth→Flower initiation→Flowering→Pollination→Fer
tilization→Fruit/seed development→Maturity            
Then a new generation begins:
Mature seed→New crop

A typical seed contains three major components:

6. Seed Coat
The seed coat is the protective outer covering of the seed.
It develops mainly from the integuments of the ovule.
Its major functions include:
- Protection against mechanical damage
- Protection against pathogens
- Reduction of excessive water loss
- Regulation of water entry
- Regulation of gas exchange
- Protection of the embryo
- Contribution to dormancy
In many seeds, the outer seed coat is called the testa.
A thinner inner layer may be called the tegmen, depending on seed structure.

## 7. Embryo
The embryo is the young plant contained inside the seed.
It contains the basic structures from which the future plant develops.
Important embryonic structures include:
- Radicle
- Plumule
- Hypocotyl

- Epicotyl
- Cotyledons

## 8. Radicle
The radicle is the embryonic root.
During germination, it is usually the first major embryonic structure to emerge.
Therefore:
Radicle→Primary root    
Its functions include:
- Establishing anchorage
- Beginning water absorption
- Beginning mineral uptake
- Establishing the root system

## 9. Plumule
The plumule is the embryonic shoot.
It gives rise to:
- Stem
- Young leaves
- Shoot apical meristem
Therefore:
Plumule→Shoot system    

## 10. Cotyledons
Cotyledons are embryonic seed leaves.
They have important functions such as:
- Storing food

- Absorbing nutrients from endosperm
- Supporting early seedling development
- Sometimes performing photosynthesis after emergence

11. Monocot vs Eudicot Seeds
A major distinction is:

Eudicot seeds
Usually have:
2 cotyledons  
Examples:
- Chickpea
- Groundnut
- Pea
- Soybean
- Bean

Monocot seeds
Have:
1 cotyledon  
In grasses, this specialized structure is called the scutellum.
Examples:
- Rice
- Wheat
- Maize
- Sorghum

12. Cereal Seed Structure
Cereal grains such as rice, wheat and maize have a specialized structure.

A generalized cereal grain contains:
- Embryo
- Endosperm
- Outer fruit/seed covering
- Scutellum
- Plumule
- Radicle
- Coleoptile
- Coleorhiza
The endosperm contains substantial food reserves, especially starch.

## 13. Coleoptile
The coleoptile is a protective sheath surrounding the young shoot in grasses.
It protects the emerging plumule as it grows upward through the soil.
This is particularly important in:
- Wheat
- Rice
- Maize
- Barley
- Other grasses

## 14. Coleorhiza
The coleorhiza is a protective sheath associated with the embryonic root of grasses.
It helps protect the radicle during early emergence.
Thus:
Coleoptile→protects young shoot   
Coleorhiza→protects young root

The seed must contain enough stored energy and nutrients to support the embryo during early
development.
Major reserves include:

Carbohydrates
Especially starch.
Common in:
- Rice
- Wheat
- Maize

Proteins
Important in:
- Pulses
- Soybean
- Legume seeds

Lipids
Important in:
- Groundnut
- Sesame
- Sunflower
- Mustard
- Soybean
Therefore:
Seed reserves=Carbohydrates+Proteins+Lipids  =   +
  +    
with the relative proportions varying greatly among crops.

## 16. Endosperm
The endosperm is a nutrient-storage tissue formed after double fertilization in typical angiosperms.
It supports embryo development and, in many species, seedling establishment.
In cereals, it is a major storage tissue.
For example:
Rice/Wheat/Maize→Large starch-rich endosperm    

17. Albuminous and Exalbuminous Seeds
Albuminous seeds
Retain substantial endosperm at maturity.
Examples include:
- Wheat
- Rice
- Maize
- Castor

Exalbuminous seeds
Most endosperm is consumed during embryo development, with reserves largely stored in cotyledons.
Examples include:
- Pea
- Chickpea
- Groundnut
- Bean
This distinction is useful for understanding seed structure and reserve location.

18. Seed Maturity
A seed passes through developmental stages before becoming a mature seed.

A simplified sequence is:
Fertilization→Embryo development→Reserve accumulation→Seed maturation      
During maturation:
- Embryo develops
- Storage compounds accumulate
- Seed dry matter increases
- Water content eventually decreases
- Desiccation tolerance may develop
- Dormancy may develop in some species

19. Physiological Maturity
Physiological maturity is approximately the stage when the seed has accumulated its maximum or
near-maximum dry matter.
Conceptually:
Physiological maturity≈Maximum seed dry matter    
After this stage, the seed may undergo drying and further maturation processes.

20. Harvest Maturity vs Physiological Maturity
These are not always identical.

Physiological maturity
The seed has essentially completed dry matter accumulation.

Harvest maturity
The seed has reached a condition suitable for practical harvesting, considering:
- Moisture
- Machinery

- Storage
- Market requirements
- Crop losses
Therefore:
Physiological maturity≠Harvest maturity    
in every situation.

21. Seed Moisture
Water content is extremely important during seed development and storage.
Young developing seeds generally contain substantial water.
As the seed matures:
Seed maturity→Moisture content generally decreases    
Many orthodox seeds eventually undergo substantial drying.

22. Orthodox and Recalcitrant Seeds
Seeds differ in their tolerance to drying.

Orthodox seeds
Can generally tolerate substantial drying and can often be stored for relatively long periods under
suitable conditions.
Examples include many cereal and pulse crops.

Recalcitrant seeds
Are sensitive to drying and may lose viability when moisture content falls too low.
Examples include seeds of some tropical tree species.
There are also intermediate behaviors between these broad categories.

23. Seed Dormancy
Seed dormancy is a condition in which a viable seed does not germinate even when some apparently
favorable environmental conditions are present.
Dormancy can prevent germination at an unsuitable time.
Thus:
Dormancy→Delayed germination    
This is biologically useful because it can prevent immediate germination during unfavorable periods.

24. Types/Causes of Seed Dormancy
Dormancy may result from:

Physical dormancy
A seed coat restricts water or gas movement.

Physiological dormancy
Internal physiological mechanisms prevent germination.

Morphological dormancy
The embryo is insufficiently developed at seed dispersal.

Combinational dormancy
More than one mechanism is involved.
Dormancy classification can be complex and species-specific.

25. Why Dormancy Is Important
Dormancy can:
- Prevent premature germination
- Synchronize germination with favorable conditions
- Improve survival
- Influence storage

- Affect seed testing
- Affect crop establishment
- Influence weed emergence

26. Seed Viability
Seed viability means the seed is alive and capable of producing a normal plant under suitable
conditions.
A seed can be:

Viable
Alive and potentially capable of germination.

Non-viable
Dead and unable to germinate normally.
Therefore:
Viability≠Germination percentage   
A viable seed may fail to germinate because of dormancy or unfavorable conditions.

27. Seed Germination Capacity
Germination is the process by which a viable seed resumes active growth and produces a seedling
under suitable conditions.
The seed stage therefore ends functionally as germination begins.
The simplified transition is:
Seed→Imbibition→Metabolic activation→Radicle emergence→Germination

Seed purity concerns whether the seed lot contains the intended crop seed without excessive:
- Other crop seeds
- Weed seeds
- Inert matter
- Other impurities
High physical purity improves planting uniformity.

32. Seed Vigor
High-vigor seeds generally show:
- Rapid germination
- Uniform emergence
- Strong seedlings
- Better establishment

- Better performance under moderate stress
Factors reducing vigor include:
- Aging
- High temperature
- Excessive moisture
- Mechanical injury
- Poor storage
- Pathogen infection

33. Seed Aging
Seeds remain alive after maturity, but their viability and vigor gradually decline during storage.
A generalized sequence is:
High-quality seed→Aging→Vigor decline→Germination decline→Loss of viability       

34. Factors Affecting Seed Longevity
Important factors include:
- Seed moisture
- Storage temperature
- Relative humidity
- Oxygen
- Initial seed quality
- Mechanical damage
- Pathogens
- Seed type/genotype
For many orthodox seeds:
Lower moisture + lower temperature→longer storage life    

within appropriate limits.

35. Seed Respiration During Storage
Seeds are living tissues and continue to respire, although the rate can become very low in dry seeds.
Generally:
Seed moisture↑→Respiration↑     
Higher respiration can result in:
- Heat generation
- Reserve depletion
- Faster deterioration
This is why moisture management is critical in seed storage.

36. Seed Storage
The objectives of seed storage are to maintain:
- Viability
- Germination
- Vigor
- Genetic identity
- Seed health
Important principles include controlling:
- Moisture
- Temperature
- Humidity
- Insects
- Rodents
- Fungi

37. Seed Dormancy vs Quiescence
These concepts are different.

Dormancy
The seed does not germinate because of internal constraints even when certain environmental
conditions appear favorable.

Quiescence
The seed does not germinate simply because an essential external condition is missing.
For example:
No water→Quiescence   whereas:
Adequate water + oxygen + temperature but internal block→Dormancy   38. Seed Imbibition
The first major event during germination is often imbibition.
Imbibition is the uptake of water by dry seed tissues.
Dry seed→Water uptake    
This causes:
- Seed swelling
- Membrane reorganization
- Metabolic activation
- Enzyme activation

39. What Happens After Water Enters the Seed?
The sequence is approximately:
Water uptake→Membrane reactivation→Respiration increases→Enzyme activation→Reserve mobiliza
tion→Cell division/expansion→Radicle emergence         
This marks the transition from the seed stage to germination.

40. Seed Enzymes
During germination, stored nutrients must be converted into forms usable by the embryo.
For example:

Starch
Broken down into soluble sugars.

Proteins
Broken down into amino acids.

Lipids
Converted into usable metabolic intermediates.
Therefore:
Stored food→Soluble/mobile nutrients→Embryo growth     

41. Role of Respiration During Seed
Germination
The embryo needs energy.
Therefore respiration increases after water uptake.
Stored reserves→Respiration→ATP→Growth    ATP   
This connects the seed stage with the previously studied topic of plant respiration.

42. Role of Photosynthesis During the Seed
Stage
A dry seed generally depends primarily on stored reserves during early germination.
After the emerging seedling develops green photosynthetic tissues:
Stored-reserve dependence→Photosynthetic independence 
  
The transition depends on the crop and developmental conditions.

43. Seed as a Source of Early Energy
Before the seedling can photosynthesize effectively:
Seed reserves→Energy + building materials→Seedling development 
   
Once leaves become functional:
Photosynthesis→New assimilates

Seeds have a temperature range for germination.
Three useful concepts are:

Minimum temperature
Below this, germination becomes very slow or stops.

Optimum temperature
Range at which germination is generally fastest and most uniform.

Maximum temperature
Above this, germination declines or may stop.
Thus:

48. Seedbed and Seed Stage
A good seedbed should provide:
- Adequate moisture
- Good seed–soil contact
- Adequate aeration
- Appropriate soil temperature
- Suitable physical structure
- Limited crusting
- Suitable depth
The objective is:

49. Seed Depth
Planting depth affects emergence.

If planted too shallow:
- Seed may dry rapidly
- Roots may establish poorly
- Seed may be exposed
If planted too deep:
- Oxygen may be limited
- Seedling may exhaust reserves before emergence
- Emergence may be delayed
Therefore optimal depth is crop- and soil-specific.

50. Seed Size
Seed size influences early seedling development.
Larger seeds often contain greater reserves.
This can provide an advantage during early establishment, especially under some stress conditions.
However:
Large seed≠automatically higher final yield   
Final crop performance depends on genetics, environment and management.

51. Seed Shape and Structure
Seeds differ greatly in:
- Size
- Shape
- Color
- Surface
- Hardness
- Reserve composition
- Dormancy

- Germination behavior
These differences can be used for:
- Identification
- Seed grading
- Variety identification
- Quality testing

52. Seed Treatment
Seed treatment is used to improve seed protection and establishment.
Possible treatments include:
- Fungicidal treatment
- Insecticidal treatment where appropriate
- Biological treatments
- Rhizobial inoculation for compatible legumes
- Micronutrient treatment
- Priming
The exact treatment should be crop-, pest- and regulation-specific.

53. Seed Priming
Seed priming is controlled hydration of seeds to initiate metabolic processes without allowing full
radicle emergence, followed by re-drying or direct sowing depending on the technique.
Potential benefits include:
- Faster germination
- More uniform emergence
- Improved early establishment
- Potential stress tolerance
Types include:
- Hydropriming

- Osmopriming
- Halopriming
- Biopriming
- Nutrient priming

54. Seed Inoculation
For legumes, inoculation with compatible Rhizobium/rhizobial bacteria can support biological
nitrogen fixation after root nodules form.
Conceptually:
Seed treatment→Beneficial microorganism→Root colonization→N fixation      
The specific microbial strain must be compatible with the crop.

55. Seed-Borne Diseases
Seeds can carry pathogens.
These may cause:
- Poor germination
- Seed rot
- Seedling disease
- Reduced emergence
- Disease introduction into new fields
Therefore seed health is an important component of seed quality.

56. Seed Damage
Mechanical damage can occur during:
- Harvest
- Threshing

- Processing
- Cleaning
- Transport
- Handling
Damage can:
- Reduce germination
- Increase pathogen entry
- Reduce vigor
- Reduce storage life

57. Seed Certification and Quality Control
Quality seed production may involve testing for:
- Genetic identity
- Physical purity
- Germination
- Moisture
- Seed health
- Other crop/weed seed contamination
The exact standards depend on crop and applicable regulatory framework.

58. Seed Rate
Seed rate is the quantity of seed planted per unit area.
It depends on:
- Crop
- Seed size
- Germination percentage
- Seed vigor
- Planting method

- Desired plant population
- Spacing
- Cultivar
- Expected field emergence
A simplified relationship is:
Required seeds≈Desired established plantsExpected establishment fraction          
For example, if desired establishment is 80%, more seeds must be planted than the desired final plant
population.

59. Germination vs Emergence
These are not identical.

Germination
Biological process in which the seed resumes growth, commonly marked by radicle emergence.

Emergence
When the developing seedling physically appears above the soil surface.
Thus:
Germination→Seedling development→Field emergence     
A seed can germinate underground but fail to emerge successfully.

Germination
## 1. Introduction
Germination is the process by which a viable seed resumes active metabolic growth under suitable
environmental conditions and develops into a seedling.
In simple terms:
Germination is the transition of a living, viable seed from a relatively inactive state to
active growth, beginning with water uptake and culminating in the emergence of the
embryonic root (radicle).
It is one of the most important stages of the crop life cycle because successful germination determines
crop establishment, plant population, uniformity, and ultimately yield potential.

Basic sequence
Mature Seed→Imbibition→Metabolic Activation→Reserve Mobilization→Radicle Emergence→Seedl
ing        

2. What Happens During Germination?
A dry seed appears inactive, but it contains:
- a living embryo
- stored food
- enzymes and cellular machinery
- genetic information
- protective tissues
When the seed encounters favorable conditions, it begins a sequence of physiological and biochemical
changes.
The major events are:
1. Water uptake
2. Seed swelling
3. Activation of metabolism
4. Respiration increases
5. Enzymes become active
6. Stored food is broken down
7. Cell division and cell expansion begin
8. Radicle emerges
9. Shoot development begins
10.Seedling becomes established

3. Germination vs Seed Formation
These are different processes.

Seed formation
Fertilization→Embryo development→Seed development→Mature seed     Germination
Mature seed→Water uptake→Embryo growth→Radicle emergence     Therefore:
Seed formation produces the seed; germination activates the seed and produces the
seedling.

4. Structure of a Seed Relevant to Germination
A seed generally contains three major components:
Seed=Embryo+Food reserves+Protective covering = + +  

A. Embryo
The embryo is the young plant inside the seed.
Important parts include:
- Radicle → develops into the primary root
- Plumule → develops into the shoot
- Hypocotyl
- Epicotyl
- Cotyledons

Radicle
The radicle is usually the first embryonic structure to emerge during germination.
It develops into the primary root and establishes early water and nutrient absorption.

Plumule
The plumule develops into the shoot system containing:
- stem
- leaves
- branches

5. Food Reserves in Seeds
The embryo needs energy before it can produce enough photosynthate through photosynthesis.
Therefore, seeds store food in the form of:

Carbohydrates
Usually stored mainly as:
- starch
- soluble sugars

Vegetative Growth
## 1. Introduction
Vegetative growth is the developmental phase in which a plant primarily increases its roots, stems,
leaves, branches, tillers, and overall vegetative biomass before or alongside the transition toward
reproductive development.
In simple terms:
Vegetative growth is the stage during which a plant builds its body—especially roots,
stems and leaves—so that it can capture water, nutrients, light and carbon and
develop the structure required for later reproduction.
The simplified crop-development sequence is:
Seed→Germination→Seedling→Vegetative Growth→Reproductive Growth→Maturity        
Vegetative growth is therefore one of the most important stages determining plant size, canopy
development, root capacity, photosynthetic potential, tillering/branching, and ultimately yield
potential.

Branching and Tillering
## 1. Introduction
Branching and tillering are important components of vegetative growth in which a plant produces
additional shoots from buds.
They increase the number of stems, shoots, leaves and potential reproductive sites of a crop.
Although the two processes are related, they are generally used for different groups of plants:
- Branching → common term for lateral shoot formation in many broadleaf/eudicot crops.

- Tillering → specialized term commonly used for the formation of basal shoots in grasses and
cereal crops.
In simple terms:
Branching is the formation of lateral shoots from axillary buds, whereas tillering is
the production of additional basal shoots (tillers), especially in grasses such as rice and
wheat.
The basic concept is:
Bud activation→Shoot initiation→Branch/Tiller development 
   

2. Position in the Crop Growth Cycle
Branching and tillering generally occur during vegetative growth:
Seed→Germination→Seedling→Vegetative Growth→Branching/Tillering→Reproductive Developme
nt         However, the exact timing differs among crops.
Some plants continue producing branches while flowering and fruiting.
Therefore:
Branching/tillering can overlap with reproductive development

- buds

## 1. Introduction
Flower initiation is the developmental process in which a plant's vegetative shoot apical meristem
changes its developmental program and begins to form a reproductive structure that will
ultimately produce flowers.

In simple words:
Flower initiation is the point at which the plant switches from primarily producing
vegetative structures such as leaves and shoots toward producing reproductive
structures.
The simplified sequence is:
Vegetative Growth→Flower Initiation→Flower Development→Anthesis/
Flowering→Pollination→Fertilization        
Flower initiation is therefore a major developmental transition in the crop life cycle.

2. What Exactly Happens During Flower
Initiation?
Before initiation, the shoot apical meristem (SAM) mainly produces:
- leaves
- leaf primordia
- axillary buds
- vegetative shoots
During flower initiation, the meristem receives developmental signals and changes its identity.
Conceptually:
Vegetative Meristem→Reproductive Transition→Inflorescence/Floral Meristem     
The meristem then begins producing structures that will eventually develop into:
- flowers
- inflorescences
- reproductive organs

3. Flower Initiation vs Flowering
These two terms are often confused.

Flower initiation
The internal developmental decision/change that begins reproductive development.

Flower development
The reproductive structures continue developing and floral organs become differentiated.

Flowering / Anthesis
The mature flower becomes visible/open and reproductive processes such as pollen release and stigma
receptivity occur.
Therefore:
Initiation≠Flowering    
A plant can have undergone flower initiation before you can see an open flower.

## 10. Photoperiodism
Photoperiodism is the response of plants to the relative duration of light and darkness.
It can strongly influence flowering in many species.
Plants are commonly categorized as:

Short-day plants
Flowering is promoted when the night exceeds a critical duration.
Examples include some varieties of:
- rice
- soybean
- sorghum

Long-day plants
Flowering is promoted under sufficiently long days or short nights.
Examples include:
- wheat
- barley
- some temperate crops

Day-neutral plants
Flowering is relatively less dependent on photoperiod.

However, these categories are simplified. Actual flowering responses depend strongly on species,
genotype, temperature and environmental conditions.

11. Critical Photoperiod
A plant may respond to a particular threshold of day/night duration.
This threshold is called the critical photoperiod.
For example, conceptually:

Self-pollination can provide:

- reliable seed production when pollinators are scarce
- maintenance of adapted genetic combinations
- relatively uniform offspring
- less dependence on external pollen sources

9. Disadvantages of Self-Pollination
Possible disadvantages include:
- reduced genetic variation
- greater expression of deleterious recessive alleles in some populations
- lower evolutionary flexibility under changing environments
- less opportunity for genetic recombination between different plants
This does not mean self-pollinated crops are inherently inferior; many are highly productive and well

11. Advantages of Cross-Pollination

- greater genetic variation
- genetic recombination

- increased opportunities for adaptation
- potential heterosis in hybrids
It can also help populations respond to changing environments.

Cross-pollination can depend strongly on:
- pollinators
- wind
- flowering synchrony
- pollen availability
- compatible plants
- suitable weather
Therefore, poor pollen transfer can reduce seed or fruit production.

Important insect pollinators include:
- honey bees
- bumblebees

- butterflies
- moths
- beetles
- certain flies
Flowers may attract insects using:
- color
- fragrance
- nectar
- pollen
- shape
- ultraviolet patterns

17. Pollination by Bees
Bees are particularly important agricultural pollinators.
When a bee visits a flower:
Flower→Pollen attaches to bee→Bee visits another flower→Pollen reaches stigma 
   This can facilitate cross-pollination.
Important crops benefiting from insect pollination include various:
- fruits
- oilseeds
- vegetables
- pulses

18. Pollination by Birds and Bats
Some specialized plants depend on animals other than insects.

Birds
Bird-pollinated flowers may have:

- bright colors
- nectar
- shapes suited to bird feeding

Bats
Bat-pollinated flowers often produce:
- abundant nectar
- strong odors
- flowers accessible at night
These systems are particularly important in ecological and horticultural contexts.

19. Water Pollination
Water pollination, or hydrophily, occurs in certain aquatic plants.
It is relatively uncommon among agricultural crops compared with wind and animal pollination.

20. Pollination Syndrome
Plants often possess combinations of floral characteristics associated with particular pollination agents.
For example:

Wind-pollinated
- abundant pollen
- reduced floral display
- exposed reproductive organs

Insect-pollinated
- attractive petals
- scent
- nectar
- specialized floral structures
This relationship can be represented as:
Flower structure↔Pollination mechanism    

21. Pollen Transfer Is Not Enough
A pollen grain landing on a stigma does not automatically mean successful reproduction.
The pollen must generally:
1. reach the stigma
2. hydrate
3. germinate
4. form a pollen tube
5. grow through the style
6. reach the ovule
7. deliver sperm cells
8. participate in fertilization
Therefore:
Successful pollination→successful pollen-pistil interaction 
  

22. Pollen Germination
Once a compatible pollen grain lands on a receptive stigma, it may absorb water and germinate.
The sequence is:
Pollen deposition→Hydration→Pollen germination→Pollen tube 
    
The pollen tube provides the pathway through which sperm cells travel toward the ovule.

23. Pollen Tube Growth
The pollen tube grows through the style toward the ovary.
Simplified:
Stigma→Style→Ovary→Ovule      
Pollen tube growth is influenced by:
- temperature

- pollen viability
- stigma condition
- genotype
- compatibility
- nutrients
- water status

24. Pollen Viability
Pollen viability refers to whether pollen is alive and capable of successfully participating in
reproduction.
Viability can be reduced by:
- excessive heat
- drought
- very low temperatures
- aging
- poor nutrition
- humidity extremes
- disease
- improper storage
Therefore:
Pollen present≠viable pollen    

25. Pollen Fertility
Pollen fertility refers to the ability of pollen to successfully effect fertilization.
A pollen grain can appear normal but still have reduced functional capacity.
Thus experimental studies may evaluate:
- pollen viability
- pollen germination
- pollen tube growth

- actual fertilization success
These are related but not identical measurements.

26. Stigma Receptivity
The stigma must be receptive for effective pollen interaction.
A receptive stigma generally:
- can support pollen hydration
- permits pollen germination
- supports pollen tube growth
The reproductive system therefore requires synchrony:
Pollen viability↔Stigma receptivity    

27. Pollen–Pistil Interaction
The stigma and style do not simply accept every pollen grain.
They can recognize biochemical compatibility.
The interaction can result in:

Compatible pollen
Pollen→Germination→Pollen tube growth    Incompatible pollen
Pollen→Recognition/rejection→Reduced pollen tube growth    This is an important mechanism for regulating fertilization.

## 28. Self-Incompatibility
Self-incompatibility (SI) is a genetically controlled mechanism in which a plant prevents or limits
fertilization by its own pollen or genetically related pollen.
Its major purpose is often to promote cross-pollination.
Conceptually:

Self pollen→Recognition→Rejection     
while:
Compatible pollen→Acceptance→Pollen tube growth     
Self-incompatibility is important in many breeding and horticultural systems.

## 29. Dichogamy
Dichogamy means that male and female reproductive functions mature at different times.

Protandry
Male function occurs first.
Anther maturity→Stigma receptivity    

Protogyny
Female function occurs first.
Stigma receptivity→Anther maturity    
Dichogamy can reduce self-pollination.

## 30. Herkogamy
Herkogamy is the spatial separation of male and female reproductive structures.
For example, if the anther and stigma are positioned so that pollen is less likely to reach the same
flower's stigma, self-pollination may be reduced.
Spatial separation→Reduced self-pollination    

31. Monoecious Plants
A monoecious plant has separate male and female flowers on the same plant.
Classic example:
- maize

Maize has:
- male tassel
- female ear
Thus:
One plant→Male flowers + Female flowers    

32. Dioecious Plants
A dioecious species has male and female flowers on separate plants.
Therefore:
Male plant≠Female plant    
Such plants necessarily require pollen transfer between male and female plants for sexual reproduction.

## 33. Cleistogamy
Cleistogamy is the production of flowers that remain closed or largely closed and undergo selfpollination within the flower.
Advantages can include:
- reproductive assurance
- reduced dependence on pollinators
It strongly favors self-pollination.

## 34. Chasmogamy
Chasmogamous flowers open and expose their reproductive structures.
They can permit:
- self-pollination
- cross-pollination
depending on the plant's reproductive system.

35. Male Sterility and Pollination
Male sterility means that the plant cannot produce functional pollen.
This can be very useful for hybrid seed production.
A simplified crossing system is:
Male-sterile parent×Pollen parent→Hybrid seed     
It reduces or eliminates the need to manually remove anthers in some crops.

36. Pollination in Hybrid Seed Production
Hybrid seed production requires controlled pollen movement.
The basic process is:
Select parents→Synchronize flowering→Prevent unwanted pollen→Transfer desired pollen→Fertilizat
ion→Hybrid seed       Methods can involve:
- emasculation
- male sterility
- controlled pollination
- isolation distances
- temporal isolation

## 37. Emasculation
Emasculation is the removal or prevention of functional anthers from a flower before pollen release.
Purpose:
Prevent self-pollination→Allow controlled pollen transfer 
  
It is widely used in plant breeding for controlled crosses.

38. Bagging and Controlled Pollination
Breeders may cover flowers with bags to prevent unwanted pollen from reaching the stigma.
A typical controlled-cross sequence is:
Select flower→Emasculate→Bag→Apply desired pollen→Re-bag 
     
The exact protocol depends on the crop.

## 1. Introduction
Seed development is the series of biological processes through which a fertilized ovule develops into a
mature seed capable of surviving, remaining viable, and eventually producing a new plant.
It begins after successful fertilization and continues through:
Fertilization→Embryo Development→Endosperm Development→Seed Filling→Maturation→Desicca
tion→Mature Seed         
The seed is one of the most important products of crop reproduction because it contains the embryonic
plant, stored reserves, and protective structures required for survival and subsequent germination.

Cellular

Helobial
An intermediate pattern with features of nuclear and cellular development.
These are botanical developmental classifications.

Maturity in Crops
## 1. Introduction
Maturity is the stage of crop development at which the plant, fruit, grain, seed, pod, tuber, or other
harvested organ has reached a sufficiently advanced physiological or developmental condition for a
particular purpose.
In agriculture, maturity does not always mean that the crop is ready to harvest immediately.
A crop can reach:
- physiological maturity
- harvest maturity
- commercial maturity
- processing maturity
- eating maturity

2. What Is Maturity?
In simple terms:
Maturity is the stage at which a crop or its harvested organ has completed the major
developmental processes required for its intended use.
For a grain crop, maturity may mean that maximum dry matter has accumulated.
For a fruit crop, maturity may refer to the stage when the fruit has developed the required size,
composition and physiological characteristics for harvest or ripening.
For a vegetable, maturity may mean reaching the desired edible size and quality.
Therefore:
Maturity depends on the crop and its intended use

Senescence in Crops
## 1. Introduction
Senescence is the natural, genetically regulated process through which a plant, plant organ, or tissue
undergoes aging, functional decline, nutrient remobilization, and eventual death.
It is not simply “the plant getting old.” Senescence is an actively regulated developmental process
involving changes in:
- photosynthesis
- chlorophyll
- protein content
- membrane function
- gene expression
- hormones
- nutrient movement
- respiration
- cellular structure
In crop production, senescence is extremely important because its timing and rate can strongly
influence yield and quality.
The general sequence is:
Growth→Reproduction→Maturity→Senescence→Death/Harvest       
However, senescence can begin in individual leaves or organs before the whole plant reaches
maturity.

2. Simple Definition
Senescence is the programmed developmental deterioration of a plant or plant organ
that leads to loss of function, nutrient remobilization, and eventually tissue death.
For a leaf:
Green leaf→Yellowing→Loss of photosynthesis→Nutrient remobilization→Leaf death       

3. Where Senescence Fits in the Crop Life Cycle

↓

↓

↓

↓

But these stages are not always completely separate.
For example, older leaves may begin senescing while fruits or grains are still developing.

4. Senescence Is Not the Same as Death
This distinction is important.

Senescence
A regulated process that leads toward loss of function and death.

Death
The final loss of cellular viability.
Thus:
Senescence→eventual tissue death   
but:
Senescence≠death itself   

5. Why Does a Plant Senesce?
Senescence serves several biological purposes.
One major purpose is resource recycling.
Older tissues can break down their stored materials and transport useful nutrients to developing organs.
For example:
Old leaf→Nutrient remobilization→Developing seed     
This can provide:
- nitrogen
- phosphorus
- potassium
- sulfur
- carbon compounds
- other nutrients
to developing reproductive organs.

6. Senescence and Nutrient Remobilization
One of the most important agricultural functions of senescence is nutrient remobilization.
For example, proteins in an aging leaf can be broken down.
Their nitrogen-containing compounds can then be transported to developing grains.

Conceptually:
Leaf protein→Protein degradation→Nitrogen-containing compounds→Phloem→Grain       
This is particularly important in cereal crops.

7. Leaf Senescence
Leaf senescence is the aging and functional decline of leaves.
Typical visible symptoms include:
1. loss of green color
2. yellowing
3. reduction in photosynthesis
4. breakdown of cellular components
5. nutrient remobilization
6. membrane deterioration
7. tissue death
The most obvious visual sign is often:
Green→Yellow→Brown    
But visible yellowing usually occurs after many biochemical changes have already started.

8. Chlorophyll Degradation
One of the major events in leaf senescence is chlorophyll breakdown.
As chlorophyll declines:
Chlorophyll↓→Green color↓     
Yellow pigments such as carotenoids may become more visible.
This causes the characteristic yellowing of aging leaves.

Photosynthetic capacity↓

- chlorophyll
- chloroplasts
- photosynthetic proteins
- electron transport components
- carbon-fixation enzymes
Eventually:
Source capacity↓   

10. Chloroplast Breakdown
Chloroplasts are among the earliest cellular structures to undergo major changes during leaf
senescence.
Processes include:
- chlorophyll degradation
- degradation of photosynthetic proteins
- changes in thylakoid membranes
- loss of photosynthetic machinery
Therefore:
Chloroplast degradation→Photosynthesis decline    

11. Protein Degradation
Aging leaves contain large quantities of proteins, particularly photosynthetic proteins.
During senescence:
Protein degradation↑

- rgans.

Older leaf→Nitrogen remobilization→Developing grain    13. Source–Sink Relationship

The leaf gradually loses source capacity but can become a source of remobilized nutrients.
Senescing leaf→Stored nutrients→Developing sink     

14. Senescence During Grain Filling
This is especially important in cereals.
The developing grain is a strong sink:
Leaf/Stem→Assimilates + Nutrients→Grain     

If senescence occurs too early:
Photosynthesis↓→Assimilate supply↓→Grain filling↓      If senescence occurs at an appropriate time, nutrients can be efficiently remobilized while productive

17. Types of Senescence
Senescence can be classified in several ways.

Whole-plant senescence
The entire plant undergoes aging and dies after completing its reproductive cycle.
Common in many annual crops.

Organ senescence
Only particular organs age.

- leaves
- flowers
- fruits
- stems

Leaf senescence
Aging and death of leaves.

Reproductive senescence
Aging of reproductive structures after their functional period.

Developmental senescence
Senescence that occurs as a normal part of the plant's developmental program.

Stress-induced senescence
Senescence accelerated by environmental or biological stress.

35. Harvesting and Crop Duration
Early-maturing varieties can provide advantages where farmers need:
- shorter crop duration
- timely planting of the next crop
- escape from late-season stress
Late-maturing varieties may provide different yield or quality characteristics depending on the crop and
environment.
Therefore crop duration should be considered within the entire cropping system.

36. Harvesting in Multiple Cropping
In multiple-cropping systems:
Crop A harvest→Land preparation→Crop B establishment     
Timely harvesting becomes especially important because delayed harvest of one crop can delay the
next crop.
For example:
Rice harvest→Wheat sowing   requires effective coordination.

37. Harvesting in Perennial Crops
Perennial crops may be harvested repeatedly without killing the whole plant.
Examples:
- mango → fruits
- guava → fruits
- tea → leaves/shoots
- coffee → berries
- coconut → nuts
- rubber → latex
Thus:

Harvest≠death of plant    
This is an important distinction from many annual crops.

38. Harvesting of Sugarcane
Sugarcane is harvested primarily for its stalks.
The process may involve:
- cutting stalks
- removing tops/leaves as appropriate
- transporting cane rapidly to processing facilities
Timeliness matters because harvested cane quality can change during delays before processing.

39. Harvesting of Cotton
Cotton is harvested for fiber-bearing bolls.
Harvest may be:
- manual
- mechanical
Important concerns include:
- boll opening
- fiber quality
- moisture
- contamination
- weather exposure
Multiple pickings may be used where bolls do not open simultaneously.

40. Harvesting of Potato
Potatoes are harvested for tubers.
Important considerations include:
- tuber maturity
- skin set

- soil condition
- mechanical damage
Harvesting in unsuitable soil moisture conditions can increase:
- tuber bruising
- clods
- mechanical damage

41. Harvesting of Groundnut
Groundnut develops pods underground.
A simplified process is:
Maturity→Digging/Lifting→Inversion/Drying→Picking/
Combining→Cleaning       
Delayed harvesting can result in pod losses because mature pods can detach from pegs or remain in
soil.

42. Harvesting of Fruits
Fruit harvesting can be:

Hand picking
Suitable for delicate/high-value fruits.

Mechanical
Suitable for crops where machinery can remove fruit efficiently without unacceptable damage.

Shaking
Used for certain tree crops where mature fruit can be detached mechanically.
Post-harvest handling must be gentle because bruising can accelerate deterioration.

43. Harvesting Tools
Common traditional tools include:
- sickle

- knife
- spade
- digging fork
- pruning shears
- harvesting baskets
Modern systems use:
- reapers
- combines
- forage harvesters
- crop pickers
- diggers
- mechanical tree shakers
- specialized horticultural harvesters

44. Combine Harvester
A combine harvester integrates several operations.
Typically:
Cutting+Threshing+Separation+Cleaning  +   +
  +    
in one machine.
It is particularly important for mechanized cereal harvesting.

45. Harvesting and Sustainable Agriculture
Efficient harvesting can contribute to sustainability by:
- reducing crop losses
- reducing repeated field operations
- improving labor efficiency
- reducing unnecessary fuel use
- improving product recovery

- reducing post-harvest waste
However, machinery should be selected according to:
- farm size
- crop
- soil condition
- available capital
- labor
- fuel/energy requirements

46. Harvesting Safety
Harvest operations can involve risks from:
- cutting tools
- moving machinery
- dust
- noise
- heat
- pesticides/residues
- heavy loads
- moving vehicles
Safe practices include:
- appropriate protective equipment
- machine guards
- proper maintenance
- safe machine operation
- avoiding unsafe contact with moving components
- proper lifting and transport procedures

49. Common Harvesting Mistakes
Harvesting too early
Can cause:
- immature seeds
- incomplete filling
- high moisture
- poor quality
- lower weight

Harvesting too late
Can cause:

- shattering
- lodging
- over-ripening
- weather damage
- pest damage
- quality deterioration

Poor handling
Can cause:
- bruising
- breakage
- contamination
- microbial deterioration

Improper drying
Can cause:
- fungal growth
- heating
- loss of viability
- reduced storage life

50. Harvesting and Seed Production
When the objective is seed production, harvesting must protect:
- genetic purity
- physical purity
- viability
- germination capacity
- seed vigor
Seed should be harvested at an appropriate maturity stage and handled carefully to avoid mechanical
damage.

51. Harvesting and Food Security
Reducing harvest and post-harvest losses effectively increases the amount of food available without
necessarily increasing cultivated area.
Conceptually:
Better harvesting→Lower losses→More recoverable food     
Therefore, harvest management is an important component of agricultural productivity.

53. The Most Important Concept
Harvest is not simply “cutting the crop.”
It is a decision and operation involving:
Correct maturity+Correct timing+Correct method+Minimum losses+Proper handling  +   +   +   +
   
A successful harvest converts the crop's biological production into recoverable economic yield.

54. Final Takeaway
Remember the entire concept as:

Maturity→Harvest Decision→Harvest Operation→Cleaning/Drying→Storage/
Processing       

Core distinctions
Maturity≠Harvest   
Harvest≠Threshing    Harvest≠Postharvest handling   
And the central agricultural objective is:
Harvest at the appropriate maturity and recover the maximum possible quantity of high-quality econom
ic product with minimum loss.
