## Module 14 — Weed Management
## 1. What Is A Weed?
Weed management begins with understanding exactly what a weed is. A weed is not simply a plant that
grows in the wrong place; its agricultural significance comes from its ability to interfere with human
- bjectives such as crop production, livestock production, water management, or ecosystem
management.

1.1 Definition of a Weed
A weed is generally defined as:
A plant growing where it is unwanted or where it interferes with a desired activity or
- bjective.
In agriculture, a more useful definition is:
Weed = An unwanted plant that interferes with crop production or resource use  
The important word is unwanted.
A plant may be desirable in one situation and considered a weed in another.

Example
Wheat is a crop in a wheat field:
Wheat in wheat field=Crop = 
But wheat plants growing accidentally in a different crop field may become unwanted:
Wheat in another crop=Volunteer/unwanted plant = 
Similarly, a grass species may be valuable as fodder in one location but considered a weed in a
vegetable field.

1.2 Weed Is a Relative Concept
There is no universal list of plants that are always weeds.
The status of a plant depends on:

Plant species + Location + Crop + Management objective + Time   
For example:
- A grass may be useful as fodder.
- The same grass may compete with wheat.
- A flowering plant may be useful for biodiversity.
- The same plant may interfere with a nursery crop.
- A volunteer crop plant may become unwanted in the following crop.
Therefore:
Weediness is contextual

1.4 Weed vs Wild Plant
A wild plant is a plant growing without deliberate cultivation.
A wild plant is not automatically a weed.
For example, a native wildflower growing in a natural habitat may be ecologically valuable.
It becomes a weed when its presence conflicts with a specific management objective.
Therefore:
Wild plant≠necessarily weed 

1.5 Weed vs Invasive Plant
These concepts are related but not identical.

Weed
A plant considered unwanted in a particular situation.

Invasive plant
A plant that spreads and establishes outside its native or intended range and can cause ecological,
economic or social impacts.
Therefore:
Not every weed is invasive   
and:
Not every invasive plant is a weed in every context   
However, invasive plants can become serious agricultural weeds.

1.6 Why Are Weeds Important in Agriculture?
Weeds compete with crops for limited resources.
The major resources include:
- Water
- Nutrients
- Sunlight
- Carbon dioxide
- Space
- Root-zone resources
Thus:
Weed→Resource competition→Reduced crop growth→Potential yield loss 

1.7 Major Characteristics of Weeds
Many successful agricultural weeds possess combinations of traits that allow them to survive and
spread effectively.
These may include:

1. High seed production
Some weeds can produce large numbers of seeds.
High seed production→Large soil seed bank 

2. Long seed persistence
Some weed seeds can remain viable in soil for years.
This creates a:
Soil weed seed bank  

3. Rapid germination
Some weeds germinate rapidly when suitable conditions occur.

4. Rapid growth
Many weeds grow quickly and compete strongly with young crops.

5. Multiple reproduction methods
Some weeds reproduce through:
- Seeds
- Rhizomes
- Stolons
- Tubers
- Bulbs
- Vegetative fragments
Therefore:

Seed+Vegetative reproduction→Strong persistence 

## 6. Dormancy
Some weed seeds remain dormant until environmental conditions become favorable.
This helps them survive unfavorable periods.

1.10 Soil Seed Bank
The soil seed bank is the population of viable weed seeds present in the soil.
Conceptually:
Existing seed bank+New seed production−Seed losses=Future seed bank 
Weed management is therefore not only about killing visible weeds.
It should also aim to:
Prevent new seeds→Reduce soil seed bank→Reduce future weed emergence

1.12 Critical Period of Weed Competition
One of the most important concepts in weed management is the critical period of weed competition.
It is the period during crop development when weed competition can cause significant yield reduction
if weeds are not adequately controlled.
Conceptually:
Critical period=Crop growth window when weed competition must be minimized 
This period is crop-, environment- and management-specific.
It is not the same for every crop.

1.13 Weeds Can Cause Yield Loss
A simplified relationship is:
Weed pressure↑→Crop competition↑→Yield potential↓ 
Yield loss depends on:
- Weed species
- Weed density
- Crop species
- Crop variety
- Time of weed emergence
- Weed size
- Crop growth rate
- Soil fertility
- Water availability
- Climate
- Duration of competition

Therefore:
Yield loss≠simply a function of weed number 
Two fields with the same number of weeds can experience different levels of crop damage.

1.14 Weeds as Hosts
Some weeds can serve as hosts or reservoirs for:
- Plant pathogens
- Insect pests
- Nematodes
Thus:
Weed→Alternative host→Pest/pathogen survival→Potential crop risk 
This is one reason weed management can also contribute to integrated pest and disease management.

### 1.15 Allelopathy
Some plants release chemical compounds that can influence the germination or growth of other plants.
This phenomenon is called:
Allelopathy  
Depending on the species and conditions, chemicals released from plants or decomposing residues
may:
- Suppress germination
- Inhibit root growth
- Alter microbial processes
- Affect neighboring plants
Allelopathy is therefore one possible mechanism of plant-to-plant interaction, although it should not be
assumed whenever plants grow poorly near another species.

1.23 Economic Threshold Concept
Not every weed must necessarily be eliminated.
The important question is whether the cost and impact of weeds justify control.
A simplified concept is:
Weed control decision=Weed damage+Control cost+Crop value+Expected yield loss 
This leads to the broader idea of an economic threshold: the weed population or expected damage
level at which control becomes economically justified.
The exact threshold varies by crop, weed species, production system and control cost.

1.30 Weed Management Philosophy
The goal is usually not necessarily to eliminate every weed.
The practical objective is:
Keep weed population below economically damaging levels 
while:
- Protecting crop yield
- Reducing weed seed production
- Preventing spread
- Minimizing environmental harm
- Managing herbicide resistance
- Maintaining sustainable production

## 2. Weed Identification
2.1 What is Weed Identification?
Weed identification is the process of determining the species or group of an unwanted plant by
examining its observable characteristics such as leaves, stems, roots, flowers, seeds, growth habit, life
cycle, habitat and other diagnostic features.

In simple terms:
Weed identification = Finding out exactly what weed is present  
It is the first technical step in effective weed management because the correct control method
depends heavily on the weed's identity and biology.

2.2 Why Is Weed Identification Important?
Different weeds have different:
- Life cycles
- Growth rates
- Root systems
- Reproductive mechanisms
- Germination patterns
- Herbicide sensitivities
- Competitive abilities
- Habitat preferences
- Control requirements
Therefore:
Correct identification→Correct biology→Correct management 
   
Misidentification can result in ineffective control.
For example:
Annual weed≠Rhizomatous perennial weed   They may require very different management strategies.

2.3 Basic Weed Identification Workflow
A practical identification process is:

Observe→Collect→Examine→Classify→Compare→Confirm        

Step 1 — Observe the field
Record:
- Crop
- Location within field
- Weed distribution
- Weed density
- Growth stage
- Soil condition
- Irrigation condition
- Season

Step 2 — Examine the plant
Look at:
- Roots
- Stem
- Leaves
- Flowers
- Fruits
- Seeds
- Underground structures

Step 3 — Determine broad group
For example:
Grass / Sedge / Broadleaf 

Step 4 — Determine life cycle
Annual / Biennial / Perennial 

Step 5 — Compare diagnostic features
Use:

- Identification keys
- Field guides
- Herbarium specimens
- Reliable agricultural databases
- Expert identification

Step 6 — Confirm
When important decisions depend on identification, confirm using more than one characteristic rather
than relying on a single visual feature.

- Amaranthus spp.

A weed life cycle describes the sequence of stages through which a weed passes from its origin or
propagule to establishment, growth, reproduction, and eventual survival or death.
Understanding the life cycle is essential because the most effective management strategy depends
heavily on when the weed emerges, how it reproduces, how long it survives, and whether it can
regenerate from underground structures.
The basic sequence is:

A weed life cycle is the period and sequence of biological development from the beginning of a weed
generation until it produces new propagules and completes or continues its survival cycle.
It can be represented as:
Establishment→Growth→Reproduction→Survival      

The major difference among annual, biennial and perennial weeds is how long this cycle takes and
whether the parent plant dies after reproduction.

## 13. Herbicides
## 1. Introduction
Herbicides are substances used to prevent, suppress, or kill unwanted plants (weeds).
They are one component of weed management and are particularly useful when weeds cannot be
controlled efficiently by hand or mechanical methods.
The basic concept is:
Herbicide→Specific physiological/biochemical effect→Weed injury→Weed suppression or death d        
Herbicides do not all work in the same way. Different herbicides interfere with different plant processes
such as:
- Photosynthesis
- Amino-acid synthesis
- Lipid synthesis
- Cell division
- Pigment formation
- Plant hormone function
- Cell membrane function
- Respiration
- Growth regulation

## 2. Definition
Exam-ready definition
A herbicide is a chemical substance or formulation used to inhibit the germination, growth,
development, or reproduction of unwanted plants or to kill them.
The word herbicide comes from:
- herbi → plant
- cide → killing
However, in agriculture, a herbicide does not necessarily kill every weed. Some herbicides merely
suppress growth or prevent reproduction.

3. Why Herbicides Are Used
Herbicides are used because weeds compete with crops for:
Water+Nutrients+Light+Space+CO2 
Uncontrolled weeds can cause:
- Reduced crop yield
- Poor crop quality
- Difficult harvesting
- Increased harvesting cost
- Increased irrigation demand
- Increased nutrient consumption
- Pest and disease habitat
- Contamination of harvested produce
- Reduced efficiency of farm machinery
Therefore:
Effective weed control→Reduced crop-weed competition→Higher crop productivity potential     

4. Herbicide Terminology
Several terms are important for understanding herbicides.

Herbicide
The weed-control chemical or active ingredient.

Active ingredient
The biologically active substance responsible for herbicidal action.

Formulation
The commercial product containing the active ingredient plus other components that improve handling,
mixing, stability, application or performance.

Selectivity
The ability to control weeds while causing acceptable injury to the crop.

Mode of action
The specific biochemical process or physiological pathway disrupted by the herbicide.

Site of action
The particular molecular target or biochemical location affected.

Contact herbicide
Primarily damages plant tissues that are directly contacted by the spray.

Systemic/translocated herbicide
Is absorbed and moved within the plant to sites where it produces its effect.

5. Selective vs Non-Selective Herbicides
5.1 Selective herbicides
A selective herbicide controls certain plant species while causing relatively little injury to a tolerant
crop when used correctly.
Weed sensitivity>Crop sensitivity >   
Examples of selectivity mechanisms may involve:
- Differential absorption
- Differential translocation
- Metabolic detoxification
- Anatomical differences
- Physiological differences

- Application timing
- Placement

5.2 Non-selective herbicides
A non-selective herbicide can injure or kill a broad range of plants.
Many plant species susceptible   
They may be used for:
- Pre-plant vegetation control
- Non-crop areas
- Field borders
- Industrial sites
- Certain fallow systems
They must be kept away from desirable crops unless the crop is specifically tolerant.

6. Contact vs Systemic Herbicides
Contact herbicides
They primarily affect the plant tissues that receive the herbicide.
Sprayed tissue→Local injury    
They can act relatively quickly.
They are generally more effective when weeds are:
- Small
- Actively growing
- Thoroughly covered by spray

Systemic herbicides
They are absorbed and transported within the plant.
Absorption→Translocation→Site of action→Plant injury      
They can be particularly useful against plants with:

- Extensive root systems
- Rhizomes
- Stolons
- Other underground regenerative structures

7. Pre-Plant Herbicides
Applied before the crop is planted.
Herbicide→Existing weeds controlled→Crop planted     
They may be useful for preparing a relatively weed-free field.

8. Pre-Emergence Herbicides
Applied before crop emergence, although exact terminology can depend on the crop and product.
The herbicide is generally present in the soil when weed seeds germinate.
Herbicide in soil→Weed germination/seedling development affected 
  
They are often particularly useful against weeds emerging from seeds.
Important: "Pre-emergence" does not always mean "before sowing." It means relative to crop/weed
emergence as defined by the product and cropping system.

9. Post-Emergence Herbicides
Applied after weeds and/or crops have emerged.
Emerged weed→Herbicide application→Weed injury

can be classified according to selectivity, timing, movement, application method and mode of

## Pre-Emergence Herbicides
## 1. Introduction
Pre-emergence herbicides are herbicides applied before the emergence of the target weed or crop,
depending on the specific use and label definition, primarily to control weeds during their
germination and early seedling establishment.
Their main purpose is to prevent weeds from becoming established and competing with the crop.
The basic sequence is:
Herbicide application→Weed germination/emergence→Herbicide uptake→Seedling injury→Weed sup
pression       
They are especially important for controlling early-season weed flushes.

## 2. Definition
Exam-ready definition
Pre-emergence herbicides are herbicides applied before the emergence of the target weed, or at a
specified crop stage before weed emergence, to prevent or suppress weed germination and early
seedling development.

Important clarification
"Pre-emergence" is a timing term. It does not automatically mean that the herbicide must always be
applied before sowing.
For example, depending on the registered use, a pre-emergence herbicide may be applied:

- Immediately after sowing
- Before crop emergence
- Before weed emergence
The specific product label and crop recommendation determine the correct timing.

3. Why Pre-Emergence Herbicides Are
Important
The early stage of crop growth is often highly sensitive to weed competition.
Early weed emergence→Competition begins→Crop growth/yield potential can be reduced     
Therefore:
Prevent early weed establishment→Reduce early crop-weed competition    
This is closely related to the critical weed-free period.

4. Main Principle
Most soil-applied pre-emergence herbicides work by creating a zone of herbicidal activity in the soil.
When weed seeds germinate and emerging tissues encounter the herbicide:
Herbicide-treated soil+Germinating weed→Herbicide uptake→Physiological disruption  +      
The weed may then:
- Fail to emerge
- Stop growing
- Develop abnormal roots/shoots
- Become chlorotic
- Die shortly after emergence

The effective zone depends on:
- Herbicide properties
- Soil texture
- Soil moisture

- Incorporation
- Rainfall/irrigation
- Weed seed depth
- Organic matter

Post-emergence herbicides are herbicides applied after the emergence of target weeds to control
- r suppress actively growing weeds.

Post-emergence = AFTER weed emergence

- Which weed species are present
- Weed density
- Weed growth stage
- Weed size
- Distribution across the field

## 1. Introduction
Herbicide resistance is one of the most important challenges in modern weed management.
When the same herbicide or the same mode of action (MOA) is repeatedly used, susceptible weeds are
controlled while naturally less-sensitive individuals survive. If those survivors reproduce, resistance
traits can become more common in the weed population.
The basic process is:

uency increases

48. Exam-Ready Answer
Spray timing is the selection of the appropriate time for herbicide application based on weed
growth stage, crop growth stage, herbicide characteristics and environmental conditions. Correct
timing is essential because small, actively growing weeds are generally easier to control, while late
application can allow greater crop–weed competition and weed seed production. Pre-emergence
herbicides are applied before target weed emergence, whereas post-emergence herbicides are

applied after weed emergence. Important timing factors include crop and weed stage,
temperature, humidity, wind, rainfall, soil moisture, irrigation, weed stress, herbicide mode of
action and rainfastness. Proper timing improves weed control, crop safety, herbicide efficiency
and resistance management.

## Herbicide Safety
## 1. Introduction
Herbicide safety refers to the practices used to protect farm workers, applicators, crops, livestock,
consumers, beneficial organisms, soil, water, and the environment from unnecessary exposure or
harm during the handling, mixing, transport, application, storage, and disposal of herbicides.
Herbicide safety is not simply about wearing protective equipment.
It is a complete management system:
Choose correctly→Handle correctly→Apply correctly→Prevent exposure→Store safely→Dispose safe
ly        

## 2. Definition
Exam-ready definition
Herbicide safety is the proper selection, handling, storage, application, transport, and disposal of
herbicides in ways that minimize risks to people, crops, animals, beneficial organisms, and the
environment.

Improper herbicide use can cause:

Human risks
- Skin exposure
- Eye exposure
- Inhalation
- Accidental ingestion
- Contaminated clothing
- Occupational exposure

Crop risks
- Crop injury
- Phytotoxicity
- Yield reduction
- Residue/carryover injury

Environmental risks
- Water contamination
- Soil contamination
- Spray drift
- Non-target plant damage
- Effects on beneficial organisms

Herbicide safety=Human safety+Crop safety+Environmental safety =

75. Exam-Ready Answer
Herbicide safety refers to the safe selection, handling, storage, transport, mixing, application and
disposal of herbicides to minimize risks to humans, crops, livestock and the environment.
Important safety practices include reading and following the product label, using the correct
registered herbicide and dose, wearing required PPE, preventing exposure during mixing and
spraying, avoiding spray drift, protecting water bodies and non-target organisms, observing reentry and pre-harvest intervals, cleaning equipment properly, and disposing of containers
according to label and local regulations. Safe herbicide use follows the principles of the right
product, right rate, right time and right place.
