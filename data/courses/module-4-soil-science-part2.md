## 1. Introduction
Soil pH is one of the most important chemical properties of soil because it strongly influences:
- nutrient availability
- nutrient toxicity
- microbial activity
- organic matter decomposition
- fertilizer efficiency
- root growth
- soil chemical reactions
- availability of phosphorus
- availability of micronutrients
- activity of beneficial microorganisms
- crop selection and productivity.

Soil pH tells us how acidic, neutral, or alkaline the soil is.

It is commonly expressed as:

6. Soil pH Is Not the Same as Soil Acidity
These terms are related but different.

Soil pH
Measures the acidity status of the soil solution.

Soil acidity
Includes the total acid-producing or acid-neutralizing condition of the soil, including acidity associated
with exchange sites and reserve acidity.

Therefore:
pH≠total acidity   
Two soils can have the same pH but require different amounts of lime to raise their pH because their
buffering capacities differ.

18. Iron and Soil pH
Iron availability generally increases as soil becomes more acidic.

Even if a nutrient is physically present in soil, the plant may not be able to access it effectively.
Therefore:
Total nutrient in soil≠plant-available nutrient

38. Soil pH and Salinity Are Different
This distinction is extremely important.

pH
Describes acidity/alkalinity.

EC
Describes electrical conductivity and is commonly used as an indicator of soluble salt concentration.
Therefore:
pH≠EC 
A soil can be:
- acidic and saline
- neutral and saline
- alkaline and saline
- acidic and non-saline.

39. Soil pH vs Sodicity
Similarly:
pH≠sodicity   
Sodicity concerns excessive exchangeable sodium and its effects on soil structure and permeability.
Some sodic soils are alkaline, but pH alone cannot diagnose sodicity.

Soil acidification is the process by which soil becomes progressively more acidic.
Important causes include:

1. Leaching of basic cations

2. Nitrogen transformations
Nitrification generates acidity.

4. Acidic inputs

Oxidation of sulfide minerals can produce strong acidity.

43. Soil Alkalization
Soil alkalization refers broadly to processes that increase soil alkalinity.
Possible causes include:
- accumulation of carbonates/bicarbonates
- limited leaching
- irrigation with certain water qualities
- accumulation of basic salts.
Sodification is related but specifically concerns sodium accumulation.

44. Buffering Capacity
Soil buffering capacity is the ability of soil to resist changes in pH when acids or bases are added.
This is extremely important.
Consider two soils:
- Soil A: low buffering capacity
- Soil B: high buffering capacity.
Adding the same quantity of lime may produce a larger pH change in Soil A than Soil B.

Thus:
Same pH≠same lime requirement   

45. What Controls Buffering Capacity?
Important factors include:
- clay content
- clay mineralogy
- organic matter
- carbonates
- exchangeable acidity.
Generally:
More reactive soil surfaces→greater buffering capacity

55. Managing Alkaline Soil
High-pH soils are more difficult to permanently acidify because of their buffering capacity, especially
when carbonates are abundant.
Management may involve:
- selecting tolerant crops/cultivars
- improving organic matter
- appropriate fertilizer strategies
- correcting specific micronutrient deficiencies
- suitable irrigation-water management
- addressing salinity/sodicity when present.
For strongly alkaline/calcareous soils, simply adding an acidifying fertilizer is not a universal solution.

## 1. Introduction
Electrical Conductivity (EC) is a measure of the ability of a soil-water extract to conduct electrical
current.
In agriculture, EC is commonly used as an indicator of the amount of dissolved ions/soluble salts
present in soil.
The basic relationship is:
More dissolved ions→higher electrical conductivity    
Therefore EC is widely used to assess:
- soil salinity
- fertilizer/salt accumulation
- irrigation-water quality
- drainage problems
- root-zone salt stress.

2. Definition of EC
Electrical conductivity of soil is the ability of the soil solution or a standardized soil
extract to conduct electrical current, which is related to the concentration and
mobility of dissolved ions.
Common agricultural units include:
dS m−1   -1  
and:
mS cm−1   -1

86. Exam-Ready Definition — EC
Electrical Conductivity (EC) is a measure of the ability of the soil solution or a
standardized soil extract to conduct electrical current due to dissolved ions. It is
widely used as an indicator of soil salinity. Increasing concentrations of soluble salts
generally increase EC, and excessive EC can cause osmotic stress, ion toxicity and
nutrient imbalance in crops.

87. Exam-Ready Definition — CEC
Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC) is the total capacity of soil to retain and exchange
positively charged ions on negatively charged surfaces of clay minerals and organic
matter. It is commonly expressed in cmolc kg⁻¹ and is an important indicator of the
soil's capacity to retain exchangeable nutrient cations such as calcium, magnesium,
potassium and ammonium.

## 36. Introduction

## 37. Definition
Soil salinity is the accumulation of soluble salts in soil, especially in the root zone, at
concentrations that can create osmotic stress, ion toxicity or nutrient imbalance and
reduce plant growth or productivity.
In simple terms:
Salt accumulation→saline soil

Important in some coastal environments.

Salt-containing aerosols can contribute in certain environments.

Mineral weathering can release ions.

93. Common Misconceptions

❌ "Organic carbon and organic matter are the same."
No.
SOC≠SOMSOC SOM

SOC is the carbon component of organic matter.

❌ "More SOC always means more immediately available nutrients."
Not necessarily.
Much of the carbon is contained in relatively stable organic matter and nutrients require
decomposition/mineralization.

❌ "High EC means high fertility."
No.
High EC indicates high ionic conductivity and may indicate harmful salinity.

❌ "Salinity and sodicity are the same."
No.
Salinity→soluble salts 
Sodicity→exchangeable sodium/structural effects 

❌ "Organic matter can remove salt from soil."
Not directly.

Organic matter can improve structure and water relations, but soluble salts generally require
appropriate leaching and drainage for removal.

❌ "A saline soil always looks white."
No.
Visible salt crusts may occur, but salts can remain dissolved and invisible.

❌ "High CEC means high nutrient fertility."
No.
CEC is capacity, not actual nutrient supply.

## Part A — Soil Sodicity
## 1. Introduction
Soil sodicity refers to a condition in which excessive sodium (Na⁺) is associated with the soil
exchange complex, causing deterioration of soil structure and restrictions on water movement, aeration
and root growth.
The most important point is:
Sodicity is primarily a sodium-related soil condition  
It is different from ordinary soil salinity.

## 2. Definition
Soil sodicity is the condition in which exchangeable sodium is sufficiently high relative
to the soil's cation exchange capacity to adversely affect soil physical properties and
plant growth.
The important word is:
Exchangeable Na+  + 
rather than simply sodium dissolved in soil water.

5. Why Does Sodium Cause a Problem?
Clay particles generally carry negative charges.
They attract positively charged ions:
Ca2+,Mg2+,K+,Na+Ca  2+ , Mg  2+ , K +, Na +
These are called exchangeable cations.
When excessive sodium occupies exchange sites:
Na+↑→clay dispersion→soil structure deterioration    
This is a major mechanism of sodic soil degradation.

6. Clay Dispersion
Clay particles are normally held together in aggregates.
Excess exchangeable sodium can increase repulsive forces between clay particles.
Consequently:
Stable aggregates→clay dispersion    
Dispersed clay can move into soil pores and block them.

7. Pore Blocking
The sequence becomes:
Na+↑→clay dispersion→pore blockage→infiltration↓      
This is why sodicity can create serious physical problems even when total soil porosity does not
initially appear extremely low.

8. Soil Structure Under Sodicity
Healthy soil generally contains stable aggregates and interconnected pores.
Sodicity can cause:
- aggregate breakdown

- clay dispersion
- surface crusting
- reduced macroporosity
- poor pore connectivity
- reduced infiltration
- poor aeration.
Thus:
Sodicity→structural degradation    

9. Sodicity and Infiltration

This creates a management challenge:
The soil needs water movement to remove salts, but sodicity has reduced the soil's ability to
transmit water.

Sodicity→poor structure→air movement↓→root aeration↓

## 2. Definition
Earthworms are segmented soil-dwelling invertebrates that feed on organic materials
and soil, burrow through the soil, and produce casts, thereby influencing soil
structure, aeration, water movement, organic matter decomposition and nutrient
cycling.
They belong to the phylum Annelida.

3. Why Are Earthworms Important?

- root growth.

Earthworms consume mixtures of:

- decomposing organic matter

- biological processes.

Soil biodiversity→biological functioning→soil health

## 1. Introduction
Decomposition is the biological and chemical breakdown of dead organic matter into simpler

The basic process is:

## 2. Definition
Decomposition is the biological and biochemical breakdown of dead plant, animal and
microbial organic matter into simpler organic and inorganic substances through the
activities of decomposers and environmental processes.
The major decomposers are:
- bacteria
- fungi.
Soil fauna also contribute by fragmenting and processing organic material.

3. Why Decomposition Is Important
Without decomposition, dead organic material would accumulate and nutrients would remain locked in
complex organic compounds.
Decomposition enables:
- nutrient recycling

- carbon cycling
- nitrogen mineralization
- phosphorus release
- sulfur cycling
- formation and transformation of soil organic matter
- microbial growth
- nutrient availability to plants.
Therefore:
Decomposition→Nutrient recycling→Plant nutrition

- fallen leaves
- dead roots
- dead insects
- animal manure
- dead microorganisms
- root exudate-derived materials
- other organic wastes.

- soluble sugars
- starch
- proteins
- cellulose

- hemicellulose
- lignin
- waxes
- oils
- minerals.

- cellulose
- hemicellulose
- lignin-associated materials.
Therefore:
Bacteria + Fungi→Major microbial decomposition    

7. Soil Fauna and Decomposition
Animals such as:
- earthworms
- termites
- mites
- springtails
- insects
- other detritivores
contribute by:
- fragmenting residues
- consuming organic matter
- mixing soil
- stimulating microbial activity.

## 8. Fragmentation
Large residues have relatively low surface area relative to their volume.
When they are fragmented:
Large residue→small particles→surface area↑      
This can increase microbial access and accelerate decomposition.
Earthworms and other soil fauna are particularly important for this process.

9. Main Stages of Decomposition
A simplified sequence is:
Plant residue→Fragmentation→Leaching→Microbial colonization→Biochemical breakdown→Minera
lization/immobilization→Humification/stabilization         
These processes can overlap rather than occur as perfectly separate stages.

## 10. Fragmentation
Physical breakdown of organic residues occurs through:
- earthworms
- insects
- termites
- mechanical disturbance
- wetting and drying
- freeze–thaw processes in some environments.
This increases the surface available to microbes.

## 11. Leaching
Water can dissolve and transport soluble compounds from residues.
Rainfall/Water→soluble organic compounds→movement into soil 
   
This is particularly important for:
- soluble sugars
- amino acids
- some mineral nutrients.

12. Microbial Colonization
Bacteria and fungi colonize organic residues.
They produce extracellular enzymes that break large molecules into smaller molecules.
For example:
Complex organic polymer→enzymessmaller molecules 
    

13. Enzymatic Breakdown
Microorganisms release enzymes such as:
- cellulases
- hemicellulases
- lignin-modifying enzymes
- proteases
- phosphatases
- lipases.
These enzymes break complex organic compounds into smaller molecules.

14. Cellulose Decomposition
Cellulose is a major component of plant cell walls.
Simplified:
Cellulose→cellulasesimple sugars        
Microorganisms then use these compounds as carbon and energy sources.

15. Lignin Decomposition
Lignin is a complex structural polymer that is relatively resistant to decomposition.
Certain fungi are particularly important in lignin transformation.
Therefore:
High lignin→slower decomposition    
in many residue systems.

## 16. Mineralization
Mineralization is the conversion of organic forms of nutrients into inorganic forms.
For nitrogen:

Organic N→NH4+   NH 4 +  
Further microbial transformation can produce:
NH4+→NO3−NH 4 +  NO 3 Therefore:
Organic nutrient→inorganic nutrient    

## 17. Immobilization
The opposite process is immobilization.
Microorganisms take inorganic nutrients from soil and incorporate them into their biomass.
For nitrogen:
NO3−/NH4+→microbial biomass N   
Thus nutrients temporarily become less available to plants.

19. C:N Ratio
One of the most important factors affecting nitrogen cycling during decomposition is the carbon-tonitrogen ratio (C:N ratio).
C:N=Carbon contentNitrogen content       

20. High C:N Residues
Examples include residues that are rich in structural carbon and relatively low in nitrogen.
Microorganisms need nitrogen to build their biomass.

If residue nitrogen is insufficient, microorganisms may take inorganic nitrogen from the soil.
High C:N residue→N immobilization risk    
This can temporarily reduce nitrogen available to crops.

21. Low C:N Residues
Nitrogen-rich organic materials generally decompose relatively quickly.
After microbial requirements are met, excess nitrogen may be released through mineralization.
Lower C:N residue→greater potential for net N mineralization 
  
Exact behavior depends on residue quality and environmental conditions.

22. Example of C:N Effect
Suppose microorganisms receive:

Residue A
C:N=80:1C:N=80:1
Microbes may require additional nitrogen from soil.
Potential result:
N immobilization  

Residue B
C:N=15:1C:N=15:1
More nitrogen is available relative to microbial carbon demand.
Potential result:
Net N mineralization  
The exact threshold is not universal and depends on residue and soil conditions.

23. Carbon Cycle During Decomposition
A simplified carbon pathway is:

Plant CO2→Plant biomass→Soil organic matter→Microbial decomposition→CO2  2     CO 2  
Some carbon becomes:
- microbial biomass
- dissolved organic carbon
- stabilized soil organic matter
- microbial residues.

24. Soil Organic Carbon Formation
Not all decomposed material immediately becomes CO₂.
Some transformed organic compounds can become associated with minerals or form more persistent
soil organic matter.
Thus:
Residue carbon→microbial products→soil organic carbon pools 
   
The persistence of soil carbon depends strongly on mineral associations, aggregation, environmental
conditions and continuing carbon inputs.

## 25. Humification
Humification refers broadly to the transformation of organic residues into more complex and stabilized
- rganic matter.
Historically, the term "humus" was used for relatively stable soil organic material.
Modern soil science recognizes multiple organic matter pools rather than treating all stable organic
matter as one uniform substance.

## 29. Temperature
Microbial activity generally increases with temperature within a suitable range.
Temperature↑→microbial activity↑→decomposition rate↑       
But extremely high temperatures can inhibit or kill organisms and dry conditions may limit activity.

## 30. Moisture
Microorganisms require suitable water availability.
Very dry soil:
Moisture↓→microbial activity↓     
Excess water can reduce oxygen.
Waterlogging→O2↓→aerobic decomposition↓   O 2    
Anaerobic decomposition may continue under saturated conditions.

## 31. Oxygen
Aerobic decomposition generally requires oxygen.
Adequate O2→efficient aerobic decomposition  2   
Under oxygen-limited conditions:
- aerobic activity decreases
- anaerobic microorganisms become more important
- decomposition pathways change
- gases such as methane may be produced in strongly anaerobic environments.

32. Soil pH
Soil pH influences:
- microbial community composition

- enzyme activity
- nutrient availability.
Therefore:
pH→microbial community→decomposition 
  

33. Residue Quality
Residues differ substantially.

Easily decomposable
- soluble sugars
- starch
- proteins.

Moderately resistant
- cellulose
- hemicellulose.

Resistant
- lignin-associated material
- waxes and certain complex compounds.
Thus:
Residue quality→decomposition rate    

34. Lignin and Decomposition
Lignin makes plant tissues structurally resistant.
High-lignin residues often decompose more slowly than residues rich in easily degradable compounds.
This is one reason:
- young green residues
- mature crop residues
- woody residues
can have very different decomposition rates.

35. C:N Ratio and Decomposition
C:N ratio affects both:
- decomposition rate
- nitrogen availability.
A high C:N ratio often results in slower net nitrogen release and greater immobilization.
A lower C:N ratio often favors faster nitrogen mineralization.

36. Particle Size
Smaller residue particles generally provide more surface area per unit mass.
Particle size↓→surface area↑→microbial access↑       
Therefore finely chopped residues often decompose faster than large intact residues under otherwise
similar conditions.

37. Soil Texture
Clay can protect some organic compounds from microbial decomposition by:
- physical protection
- mineral association
- aggregate formation.
Thus decomposition can differ between:
- sandy soil
- loamy soil
- clay-rich soil.

38. Soil Aggregation and Decomposition
Organic matter enclosed within aggregates may be physically protected.

Aggregate protection→microbial access↓→decomposition of protected C↓       
This contributes to soil carbon persistence.

39. Microbial Community
Different microorganisms specialize in different substrates.
For example:
- bacteria may rapidly utilize simple compounds
- fungi can efficiently process complex plant materials
- specialized microorganisms transform particular nutrients.
Therefore:
Microbial diversity→different decomposition capabilities

Broad breakdown of organic matter.

64. Exam-Ready Definition
Decomposition is the biological and biochemical breakdown of dead plant, animal and
microbial organic matter into simpler organic and inorganic compounds through the
activities of microorganisms and soil fauna. It is a fundamental process of carbon and
nutrient cycling and contributes to soil organic matter formation, nutrient
mineralization and soil fertility.
