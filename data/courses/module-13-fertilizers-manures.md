MODULE 13 — Fertilizers & Manures
Module 13 deals with how nutrients are supplied to crops through organic materials, chemical
fertilizers, biofertilizers, and different application methods.
The central idea is:
Nutrient Source+Right Rate+Right Time+Right Place+Right Method→Efficient Crop Nutrition + + + + 
  
A complete nutrient-management system does not simply mean “adding fertilizer.” It means supplying
the right nutrients in the right chemical form and amount, at the right growth stage and location,
while minimizing nutrient losses and maintaining soil health.

1. Fundamentals of Fertilizers and Manures
1.1 What is a fertilizer?
A fertilizer is a material, natural or manufactured, that is applied to soil, plants, or irrigation water to
supply one or more essential plant nutrients in a form available or potentially available to plants.
Examples:
- Urea → mainly nitrogen
- DAP → nitrogen + phosphorus
- MOP → potassium
- SSP → phosphorus + sulfur
- NPK fertilizer → nitrogen + phosphorus + potassium
- Zinc sulfate → zinc

Simple definition
Fertilizer is a nutrient-supplying material used to correct or prevent nutrient deficiencies
and support crop growth and productivity.

1.2 What is manure?
Manure is an organic material derived mainly from plant, animal, or biological sources that is added to
soil to supply nutrients and improve soil properties.
Examples:
- Farmyard manure
- Compost
- Vermicompost
- Green manure
- Crop residues
Manures generally contain nutrients in lower and more variable concentrations than concentrated
chemical fertilizers.
However, they can provide important benefits to:
- soil organic matter
- soil structure
- water-holding capacity
- microbial activity
- nutrient cycling
- aggregation

## Part A — Organic Sources
2. Farmyard Manure (FYM)
### 2.1 Definition
Farmyard manure (FYM) is a decomposed mixture of livestock dung, urine, bedding materials, and
- ther farm wastes that is collected and stored for use as an organic soil amendment and nutrient source.
Typical components include:
- cattle dung
- urine
- straw
- bedding material
- fodder residues
- farmyard organic wastes

### 2.2 Composition
FYM does not have one fixed nutrient composition.
Its nutrient content depends on:
- animal species
- feed
- bedding material
- storage method
- decomposition
- moisture content
- urine collection
- age of manure
- handling losses
Therefore:
FYM composition is highly variable

- nitrogen

- nutrient cycling

- microbial activity
- decomposition

## 3. Compost
### 3.1 Definition
Compost is a stabilized organic material produced through controlled biological decomposition of
plant and/or animal residues under suitable moisture, oxygen, temperature and microbial conditions.
Basic process:

Organic Waste→Microbial Decomposition→Stabilization→Compost 

3.2 Composting process
Microorganisms break down organic materials.
The process can be simplified as:
Organic Matter+O2→CO2+H2O+Heat+Stable Organic MatterOrganic\ Matter + O 2  CO 2+H 2O+Heat+Stable\ Organic\ Matter
Temperature commonly rises because microbial decomposition releases heat.

3.3 Composting stages
1. Initial/mesophilic stage
Moderate-temperature microorganisms become active.

2. Thermophilic stage
Temperature increases substantially.
Rapid decomposition occurs.

3. Cooling stage
Easily decomposable materials become depleted and temperature decreases.

4. Maturation stage
More stable organic compounds develop and the compost becomes more mature.

3.4 Important composting factors
Carbon:Nitrogen ratio
Microorganisms require carbon and nitrogen.
Very high C:N materials can cause:
N Immobilization 
during early decomposition.

Moisture
Too dry:
Microbial activity↓Microbial\ activity Too wet:
O2↓O 2 and anaerobic conditions can develop.

Oxygen
Aerobic composting requires oxygen.

Particle size
Smaller particles generally decompose faster because surface area increases, but excessive fine material
can restrict aeration.

Temperature
Temperature reflects microbial activity and composting progress.

3.5 Mature compost
Good mature compost generally has:
- stable organic matter
- reduced easily decomposable material
- earthy appearance/odor
- relatively stable temperature
- reduced phytotoxicity compared with immature material

### 3.6 Benefits
Compost can:
- add organic matter
- supply nutrients
- improve aggregation

- improve water retention
- support microbial activity
- improve soil structure
- contribute to nutrient cycling
- recycle farm/household organic wastes

## 4. Vermicompost
### 4.1 Definition
Vermicompost is a stabilized organic material produced through the interaction of earthworms and
microorganisms during decomposition of suitable organic wastes.
Core process:
Organic Waste→Earthworms+Microorganisms→Vermicompost 

4.2 Role of earthworms
Earthworms:
- consume partially decomposed organic material
- fragment organic matter
- increase surface area
- mix material

- produce casts
- stimulate microbial activity
The final material is a biologically active organic amendment.

### 4.4 Nutrients
Vermicompost can supply:
- N
- P
- K
- Ca
- Mg
- S

### 4.5 Benefits
- organic matter addition
- nutrient cycling

- improved water-holding characteristics
- recycling of organic waste
- potential improvement in nutrient availability

### 4.6 Limitations
- Requires suitable production conditions.

- Bulky compared with chemical fertilizers.
- Nutrient composition varies.
- Poor-quality feedstock can introduce contaminants.
- Not a substitute for all crop nutrient requirements.

5. Green Manure
### 5.1 Definition
Green manure refers to a crop or plant biomass grown primarily to be incorporated into the soil while
it is still green or relatively succulent, with the purpose of improving soil fertility, organic matter and
nutrient cycling.
Core:
Green Manure=Grow Crop→Biomass Production→Soil Incorporation 

5.2 Common green-manure crops
Important examples include:
- sunn hemp (Crotalaria juncea)
- dhaincha (Sesbania spp.)
- sesbania
- sunhemp
- certain legumes and other suitable cover crops
Legumes are especially valuable because of biological nitrogen fixation.

5.3 Nitrogen contribution
Legume:

### 5.4 Benefits
Nutritional
- nitrogen contribution
- nutrient recycling

Physical
- aggregation
- organic matter
- infiltration
- water retention

Biological
- microbial activity
- soil biodiversity
- decomposition

Weed management
A dense green-manure crop can suppress weeds through:
- competition
- shading
- ground cover

Erosion protection
Living vegetation protects soil from:
- raindrop impact
- runoff
- wind erosion

### 5.5 Limitations
- Requires land/time.
- Biomass production requires water and nutrients.

- Decomposition can temporarily immobilize N if material has a high C:N ratio.
- Incorporation requires appropriate machinery/labor.
- Poorly selected species may harbor pests or diseases.
- Timing is important.

5.6 Green manure vs cover crop
They can overlap.
Cover crop emphasizes soil cover and ecosystem functions.
Green manure specifically emphasizes biomass incorporation into soil.
Therefore:
Some green-manure crops are cover crops, but not every cover crop is used as green manure.

## 7. Biofertilizers
### 7.1 Definition
A biofertilizer is a preparation containing living microorganisms that, when applied appropriately to
seed, soil, roots or planting material, can contribute to plant nutrient availability or nutrient acquisition
through biological processes.
Important distinction:
Biofertilizer≠Organic manure   
Biofertilizers are primarily microbial inoculants.

7.2 Major types
Nitrogen-fixing organisms
Examples:
- Rhizobium / related rhizobia
- Azotobacter
- Azospirillum
- cyanobacteria in appropriate systems

Phosphate-solubilizing microorganisms
PSB/PSM-type inoculants can help mobilize poorly available phosphorus.

Mycorrhizal inoculants
Especially arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF).

Potassium-solubilizing microorganisms
Certain microbes can contribute to mobilization of mineral-associated potassium.

7.3 Rhizobium and legumes
Classic symbiosis:
Legume+Rhizobium→Root Nodules→Biological N Fixation 
Atmospheric nitrogen:
N2→NH3/NH4+N 2  NH 3/NH 4 +
Nitrogen is then incorporated into plant compounds.

### 7.4 Azotobacter
Azotobacter is a free-living nitrogen-fixing bacterium associated mainly with non-leguminous cropping
systems.
Its effectiveness depends on:
- soil environment
- carbon availability
- population establishment
- crop
- formulation
- management

### 7.5 Azospirillum
Azospirillum is associated with plant roots and can contribute to biological nitrogen fixation and plantgrowth-promoting effects.

It is commonly discussed in relation to:
- cereals
- grasses
- other non-leguminous crops

7.6 Phosphate-solubilizing microorganisms
Some microorganisms produce:
- organic acids
- enzymes
- chelating compounds
These can increase mobilization of poorly available phosphorus.
Conceptually:
Fixed/insoluble P→Microbial activityMore available P 
    

### 7.7 Mycorrhiza
Mycorrhizal fungi form associations with plant roots.
General relationship:
Plant Root↔Mycorrhizal Fungus 
Plant supplies carbon to fungus.
Fungus can expand the effective soil-exploration network and improve acquisition of nutrients,
especially phosphorus, and sometimes water.

### 7.8 Benefits
Biofertilizers can:
- contribute to nutrient availability
- support biological N fixation
- mobilize phosphorus

- improve nutrient acquisition
- support root growth
- interact with rhizosphere microbes
- reduce dependence on some external nutrient inputs in suitable situations

### 7.9 Limitations
Effectiveness depends strongly on:
- microbial strain
- crop
- soil pH
- temperature
- moisture
- organic matter
- salinity
- native microbial community
- inoculant quality
- storage
- application method
Therefore:
Biofertilizer performance is environment-dependent  
Biofertilizers should not automatically be treated as complete replacements for mineral fertilizers.

14. Basal Application
Definition
Basal application is the application of fertilizer before sowing/planting or at the time of
sowing/planting so that nutrients are available during early crop establishment.
Basal = Before or at planting  

### 14.1 Purpose
Provides nutrients during:
- germination
- root establishment
- early vegetative growth
Especially useful for nutrients that support early development, depending on soil/crop.

14.2 Typical nutrients
Phosphorus is often strategically applied near planting because:
- it is relatively immobile in soil
- early root development can be important
Potassium may also be applied basally depending on soil/crop.
Nitrogen may be partly basal but often needs splitting because of mobility and loss risks.

### 14.3 Advantages
- Early nutrient availability.
- Supports establishment.

- Efficient placement possible.
- Useful for immobile nutrients.

### 14.4 Risks
Too much fertilizer close to seed can cause:
Salt injury / ammonia injury / poor germination  
Therefore:
Seed + fertilizer placement must be managed carefully  

15. Top Dressing
Definition
Top dressing is the application of fertilizer to an established crop after emergence, generally on or near
the soil surface.
Top Dressing = Fertilizer applied after crop establishment  

15.2 Main purpose
Most commonly used for nutrients requiring additional supply during crop growth, particularly
nitrogen.
For example:
Urea→N supplyUrea N\ supply
during active vegetative/reproductive growth.

### 15.3 Advantages
- Matches nutrient supply with crop demand.

- Reduces excessive early nutrient availability.
- Can improve N-use efficiency.
- Allows adjustment based on crop condition/weather.

### 15.4 Limitations
Surface-applied urea may experience:
NH3 volatilization 
if conditions favor loss.
Rain or irrigation may help move nutrients into the soil, but excessive rainfall can increase other losses.

## 1. Introduction
Farmyard manure (FYM) is one of the most important traditional organic manures used in
agriculture. It is primarily made from decomposed livestock dung, urine, bedding/litter material,
and other farmyard organic wastes.
FYM is important because it performs two functions simultaneously:
Nutrient Supply+Soil Improvement +  
Unlike concentrated chemical fertilizers, FYM generally contains nutrients in relatively low and
variable concentrations, but it contributes significantly to soil organic matter, nutrient cycling, soil
structure, water retention, and biological activity.

Exam-ready definition
Farmyard manure is a partially or well-decomposed mixture of livestock dung, urine,

Animal waste + bedding + decomposition→FYM

- soluble compounds

Organic Substrate→Microbial Growth→HeatOrganic\ Substrate  Microbial\ Growth
 Heat

- rapid decomposition occurs
- proteins and carbohydrates are broken down
- many weed seeds and some pathogens can be reduced if suitable temperature-time conditions
are achieved
- large amounts of heat are produced

Important

- organic matter becomes more stable
- phytotoxic compounds can decline
- nutrient transformations continue
- humified/stable organic fractions develop

Mature, stabilized compost

Vermicompost
## 1. Introduction
Vermicompost is a stabilized organic material produced through the biological processing of organic
wastes by earthworms together with microorganisms.
Earthworms consume, fragment, mix and transform organic material, while microorganisms carry out
much of the actual biochemical decomposition.

The core idea is:
Organic Waste+Earthworms+Microorganisms→Vermicompost 
Vermicompost is used in agriculture primarily as an organic manure and soil amendment. It can
contribute nutrients while improving soil biological and physical properties.

## 2. Definition
Exam-ready definition
Vermicompost is a stabilized, finely divided organic material produced through the
biological processing and decomposition of suitable organic wastes by earthworms
and associated microorganisms.
In simple terms:
Earthworms + organic waste + microorganisms → Vermicompost

4. Basic Principle
Vermicomposting combines two major biological processes:

Earthworm processing
Earthworms:
- consume organic material
- fragment it
- mix it
- increase surface area
- produce casts

- alter microbial habitats

Microbial decomposition
Microorganisms:
- decompose organic compounds
- mineralize nutrients
- stabilize organic matter
- transform carbon and nitrogen compounds
Thus:
Earthworm Activity+Microbial Decomposition→Stabilized Organic Material 

5. Role of Earthworms
Earthworms are the central biological agents in vermicomposting.
They process organic material through their digestive system.
A simplified pathway is:
Organic Waste→Earthworm Consumption→Fragmentation+Digestion→Casts→Microbial Transforma
tion→Vermicompost 
Earthworms do not perform all decomposition themselves. Microorganisms remain extremely

- consume organic residues

- tolerate relatively dense populations
- process organic material efficiently
Not every earthworm species is equally suitable for vermicomposting.

Green Manure
## 1. Introduction
Green manure is a crop or plant biomass that is grown specifically for incorporation into the soil
while it is still relatively green and succulent, rather than being harvested primarily for grain, food, or
sale.
Its main purpose is to improve:
- soil organic matter
- nutrient cycling
- nitrogen supply, especially when legumes are used
- soil structure
- microbial activity
- water infiltration and retention
- soil fertility
- long-term soil productivity
The fundamental concept is:
Grow Biomass→Incorporate into Soil→Decomposition→Nutrient Recycling + Soil Improvement d        

## 2. Definition
Exam-ready definition
Green manure is a crop or plant biomass grown and incorporated into the soil while
green and relatively succulent, primarily to improve soil fertility, organic matter,
nutrient cycling and soil physical and biological properties.
In simple words:
Grow a crop mainly to feed the soil  

3. Why Is It Called "Green" Manure?
It is called green manure because the plant material is generally incorporated while it is:
- green
- succulent
- actively growing
- relatively rich in easily decomposable organic compounds
It does not necessarily mean the crop must be literally bright green at the exact time of incorporation.
The important idea is:
Fresh/green biomass→soil incorporation    

4. Green Manure vs Green Manure Crop
These terms are closely related but useful to distinguish.

Green manure crop
The crop specifically grown for biomass incorporation.

Green manure
The biomass/material incorporated into the soil.
For example:
Sesbania grown→cut/incorporated→green manure    5. Major Types of Green Manure
Green manure can broadly be classified into:
1. Leguminous green manure
2. Non-leguminous green manure
3. In-situ green manure
4. Green leaf manure

6. Leguminous Green Manure
Legumes are especially valuable because many form symbiotic associations with nitrogen-fixing
bacteria.
Examples include:
- sunn hemp — Crotalaria juncea
- dhaincha — Sesbania spp.
- Sesbania — Sesbania aculeata / related species depending classification
- green gram — Vigna radiata
- cowpea — Vigna unguiculata
- cluster bean/guar — Cyamopsis tetragonoloba
- certain clovers
- vetches
- other suitable legumes
The exact species used depends on climate, soil, cropping system and region.

7. Nitrogen Fixation in Green Manure Legumes
This is one of the most important concepts.
Legume roots may form nodules containing nitrogen-fixing bacteria.
The simplified pathway is:
Atmospheric N2→Biological Nitrogen Fixation→Reduced N→Plant Biomass 
The plant does not directly use atmospheric N2N 2 through ordinary root uptake.

Instead, symbiotic microorganisms such as rhizobia convert atmospheric nitrogen into biologically
usable forms.

8. Green Manure and Nitrogen Supply
When the green manure is incorporated:
Plant Biomass→Decomposition→Organic N→NH4+→NO3−→Plant Uptake 
Therefore:
Legume green manure can contribute biologically fixed nitrogen to the soil system.
   
But an important point is:
Nitrogen fixed≠Nitrogen immediately available to the next crop 
  
Some nitrogen remains in:
- soil organic matter
- microbial biomass
- undecomposed residues
and becomes available progressively.

9. Non-Leguminous Green Manure
Non-legumes can also be used as green manure.

- certain grasses
- cereal biomass
- other rapidly growing cover crops
Their main contributions can include:
- organic carbon
- soil cover
- nutrient recycling

- erosion control
- biomass addition
However, they generally do not provide the same direct biological nitrogen-fixation pathway as
leguminous green manures.

Green leaf manure involves collecting green leaves and tender branches from suitable trees/shrubs
and incorporating them into agricultural soil.
Examples may include suitable:
- leguminous trees
- shrubs
- locally adapted biomass sources

In-situ green manuring means the green manure crop is grown and incorporated in the same field

2. Exam-Ready Definition
Crop residues are the plant materials remaining after harvesting or processing a crop,
including straw, stalks, leaves, roots, husks, cobs, pods, shells, stubble and other nonmarketable or unused plant parts.
In simple terms:
Crop harvested→useful product+remaining biomass   +   
The remaining biomass is the crop residue.

3. Crop Residue Is Not the Same as Agricultural
Waste
The word waste can be misleading.
A crop residue may have economic and ecological value.
For example:

Crop Residue→Soil Amendment+Animal Feed+Compost+Bioenergy+Industrial Raw Material 
Therefore:
Crop residue becomes waste mainly when it has no planned beneficial use or is
improperly disposed of.

4. Where Do Crop Residues Come From?

A. Field residues
Materials left in the field after harvesting.
Examples:
- rice straw

- roots
- stubble

B. Processing residues
Materials produced during processing.
Examples:
- rice husk
- rice bran
- maize cobs
- groundnut shells
- cottonseed residues
- sugarcane bagasse
- oilseed press residues

Therefore:
Crop Residues=Field Residues+Processing Residues

## 9. Cellulose
Cellulose is a major structural carbohydrate.
It is composed mainly of glucose units linked into long chains.
Simplified:

Cellulose→Microbial Decomposition→Simpler Carbon Compounds 
Cellulose can be decomposed by specialized microorganisms and enzymes.

## 10. Hemicellulose
Hemicellulose is another group of structural polysaccharides.
It is generally more readily decomposed than lignin.
Hemicellulose→Microbial Breakdown→Simpler Compounds 

## 11. Lignin
Lignin is a complex aromatic polymer associated with plant structural tissues.
It is relatively resistant to decomposition.
Therefore:
Lignin↑→Decomposition Rate↓ 
This is one reason mature stalks and straw can decompose slowly.

12. C:N Ratio of Crop Residues
The carbon-to-nitrogen ratio (C:N) is one of the most important characteristics of crop residues.
C:N=CarbonNitrogen  Nitrogen   
Residues with:

High C:N
Usually decompose more slowly and may cause temporary nitrogen immobilization.
High C:N→Microbial N Demand↑→N Immobilization Tendency↑ 

Lower C:N
Generally decompose more rapidly.
Lower C:N→Faster N Mineralization Potential 

13. Nitrogen Immobilization
Suppose a large amount of high-carbon residue is incorporated.
Microorganisms require nitrogen to build their cells.
If the residue does not contain enough nitrogen, microorganisms may take mineral nitrogen from the
soil.
High C:N Residue→Microbial N Demand↑→Soil Mineral N temporarily unavailable 
This is called nitrogen immobilization.

14. Nitrogen Mineralization
As residue decomposition progresses, nitrogen contained in organic matter can become mineralized.
Organic N→NH4+→NO3−→Plant Uptake 
Therefore residue decomposition can eventually contribute nitrogen to the crop-soil system.

Potential risk

- pathogens
- disease inoculum
Therefore residue management should consider the crop's pest and disease history.

38. Crop Residue Burning
Open burning is a disposal practice in which residues are intentionally burned in the field.
It can rapidly remove bulky material but has environmental and soil consequences.

Combustion converts much of the carbon into gases and leaves a smaller ash fraction.
Residue Burning→Rapid C Loss+Smoke/Particulate Emissions+Nutrient Losses 
Some mineral nutrients may remain in ash, but others can be lost through volatilization or
smoke/particulate transport.

39. Why Burning Is Problematic
Potential problems include:

Air pollution
Production of:
- particulate matter
- carbon monoxide
- nitrogen oxides
- other combustion products

Carbon loss
Much of the organic carbon is rapidly converted to gases.

Biological loss
Soil surface organisms can be affected by intense heat.

Nutrient loss
Some nutrients can be lost during burning.

Soil effects
Repeated intense burning can negatively affect soil biological and physical properties.
Therefore:
Burning converts a potentially useful biological resource into rapid emissions and ash.

## Biofertilizers
## 1. Introduction
Biofertilizers are preparations containing living microorganisms that, when applied to seeds, plants,
soil, or other suitable planting materials, help improve plant nutrient availability and nutrient-use
efficiency through biological processes.
They are especially important in sustainable agriculture, organic nutrient management, integrated
nutrient management (INM), soil health management, and reduction of excessive dependence on
chemical fertilizers.
A biofertilizer does not simply mean “natural fertilizer.” Its defining feature is the use of beneficial
living microorganisms whose biological activity contributes to plant nutrition.

Core concept
Beneficial Microorganisms+Suitable Host/Soil Conditions→Improved Nutrient Availability +    
Examples include:
- Rhizobium → biological nitrogen fixation in legumes
- Azotobacter → associative/free-living nitrogen fixation
- Azospirillum → associative nitrogen fixation, especially with cereals and grasses
- Phosphate-solubilizing microorganisms (PSM) → improve availability of poorly soluble
phosphorus
- Mycorrhiza/AM fungi → improve phosphorus and micronutrient acquisition and root
exploration
- Potassium-solubilizing microorganisms → help release some soil K from less-available
mineral forms
- Zinc-solubilizing microorganisms → improve Zn availability in suitable conditions
- Cyanobacteria → nitrogen fixation, particularly important in some flooded rice systems

Certain microorganisms convert atmospheric nitrogen into biologically usable nitrogen compounds.

N2→Reduced N compounds→Plant N nutritionN 2   The enzyme nitrogenase is central to biological nitrogen fixation.

12.1 How do they solubilize phosphorus?
Several mechanisms are involved.

Organic acid production

      Chelation

13. Phosphate-Mobilizing Microorganisms
Phosphate solubilization and phosphate mobilization are related but not identical.

Solubilization
Converts relatively insoluble phosphorus into more soluble forms.

Mobilization
Helps plants access phosphorus that may already be present in the soil but is spatially or chemically
difficult to acquire.
Mycorrhizal fungi are particularly important for phosphorus acquisition.

## Fertilizers

## 5. Npk Fertilizers
5.1 What are NPK fertilizers?
NPK fertilizers are fertilizers that supply one or more of the three primary plant nutrients:
- N — Nitrogen

- P — Phosphorus
- K — Potassium
In commercial fertilizer grades, the numbers are normally expressed as:
N−P2O5−K2O 
For example:
10−26−26 
means approximately:
- 10% N
- 26% P₂O₅
- 26% K₂O
It does not mean 10% N + 26% elemental P + 26% elemental K.

5.2 Why are NPK fertilizers important?
Plants require N, P and K in relatively large quantities.

Nitrogen
Mainly associated with:
- Vegetative growth
- Chlorophyll
- Proteins
- Amino acids
- Enzymes
- Leaf development
N→Growth+Proteins+Chlorophyll 

Phosphorus
Important for:
- Energy transfer
- ATP-related metabolism
- Root development
- Nucleic acids

- Reproduction
- Early establishment
P→Energy+Roots+Reproduction 

Potassium
Important for:
- Enzyme activation
- Stomatal regulation
- Water relations
- Stress response
- Carbohydrate transport
- Crop quality
K→Water+Enzymes+Stress+Quality 

5.3 Understanding NPK fertilizer grades
The fertilizer grade follows:
N−P2O5−K2O 

Example: 10-10-10
Contains approximately:
10%N+10%P2O5+10%K2O10\%N+10\%P 2O 5+10\%K 2O

Contains:

Contains:

Example: 20-20-0
Contains:
20%N+20%P2O5+0%K2O20\%N+20\%P 2O 5+0\%K 2O
So:

The grade tells you the nutrient concentration, not the recommended dose.  

5.4 Types of NPK fertilizers
NPK fertilizers can be classified in several ways.

A. Compound fertilizers
Different nutrients are chemically combined within the fertilizer granule.
For example, a compound fertilizer may contain:
N+P+KN+P+K
in each granule.

Advantage
More uniform nutrient distribution when correctly applied.

B. Complex fertilizers
Nutrients are incorporated through chemical manufacturing processes into a compound fertilizer.
They can contain combinations such as:
N+P+KN+P+K
and sometimes secondary/micronutrients.

C. Physical blends
Different fertilizer granules are physically mixed.
For example:
Urea+DAP+MOPUrea+DAP+MOP
can be blended to achieve a desired nutrient ratio, provided the materials are compatible and the blend
is properly formulated.

Important distinction

Reproductive/fruiting stage

## Application Methods
Fertilizer application means placing or delivering a fertilizer nutrient into the soil, onto the plant,
- r through irrigation water in a way that allows the crop to obtain the nutrient efficiently while
minimizing losses.
The two fundamental timing-based methods are:
1. Basal application
2. Top dressing
These methods are especially important for understanding when a fertilizer should be supplied.

## 1. Basal Application
### 1.1 Definition
Basal application is the application of fertilizer before sowing/planting or at the time of
sowing/planting, so that nutrients are available to the crop during early establishment and subsequent
growth.
In simple terms:
Basal application = Fertilizer supplied at the beginning of crop establishment  
It may be applied:
- Before sowing
- During sowing
- At planting
- During transplanting
- Just before planting

1.2 Purpose of basal application
The major purpose is to ensure that essential nutrients are available in the root zone when the young
crop begins active growth.
The sequence is:
Fertilizer application→Nutrient availability→Root uptake→Early crop establishment 
Basal fertilizer can support:
- Root establishment
- Early vegetative growth
- Initial nutrient supply
- Seedling establishment
- Early crop vigor
- Early reproductive preparation

1.3 Why is basal application important?
Immediately after germination or transplanting, the plant begins developing its root system.
If an essential nutrient is unavailable during this period, early growth can be restricted.
Therefore:
Good early nutrition→Good root development→Better crop establishment 
However, fertilizer should not simply be placed directly against seeds or young roots unless the product
and application method are specifically suitable.

1.4 Nutrients commonly suitable for basal
application
Basal application is particularly important for nutrients that:
- Are required early
- Are relatively immobile in soil
- Need to be positioned near developing roots

Phosphorus
Phosphorus is a classic example.
Phosphate ions are relatively immobile in many soils.
Therefore:
P fertilizer→Early placement→Near developing root zone 
This is one reason P fertilizers such as DAP and SSP are commonly incorporated or placed near the
root zone.

Potassium

- Crop
- Soil
- K requirement

- Soil testing
- Fertilizer source
- Irrigation
- Crop growth pattern
MOP is a common K source.

4. Incorrect timing

Applying a fixed fertilizer dose without soil testing can cause nutrient imbalance.

## 7. Split Application
### 7.1 Definition
Split application is the practice of dividing the total required fertilizer dose of a nutrient into two or
more applications at different times during the crop-growing period, rather than applying the entire
quantity at once.
For example, if a crop requires:
120 kg N/ha120\ kg\ N/ha
instead of applying all 120 kg at one time:
120120
it may be divided into several applications:
40+40+40=120 kg N/ha40+40+40=120\ kg\ N/ha

The actual split depends on the crop, soil, climate and recommendation.
Split application = Divide the total fertilizer dose according to crop demand   

7.2 Why is split application needed?
Crop nutrient demand is not constant throughout the crop life cycle.
A simplified pattern is:
Seedling→Vegetative growth→Flowering→Fruit/Grain filling→MaturitySeedling  Vegetative\ growth  Flowering  Fruit/Grain\ filling  Maturity
Nutrient demand changes across these stages.
Therefore:
Nutrient demand changes→Fertilizer supply should be synchronized 

7.3 Main objective
The main objective is:
Synchronize nutrient availability with crop nutrient demand   
This can potentially:
- Improve nutrient-use efficiency
- Reduce nutrient losses
- Reduce unnecessary nutrient accumulation
- Improve crop growth
- Improve fertilizer recovery
- Reduce environmental losses

## 8. Integrated Nutrient
## Management (Inm)
### 8.1 Definition
Integrated Nutrient Management (INM) is the planned and balanced use of organic,
inorganic/mineral, biological and recycled nutrient sources, together with appropriate soil and crop
management, to maintain soil fertility and supply plant nutrients efficiently and sustainably.
In simple terms:
INM = Manage all suitable nutrient sources together   

8.2 Core concept
Instead of depending entirely on one source:

8.3 Why is INM needed?
No single nutrient source is perfect.

Chemical fertilizers
Advantages:
- Concentrated
- Predictable nutrient content
- Rapid nutrient availability for many products

- Can be lost
- Do not replace organic matter
- Excessive use can create nutrient imbalance

Organic manures

- Improve soil biological activity
- Supply nutrients gradually

- Nutrient concentration is usually lower
- Nutrient content varies
- Large quantities may be needed

Biofertilizers
Advantages:

- Can improve nutrient acquisition

- Performance depends strongly on environmental conditions
- They are not guaranteed substitutes for all fertilizer requirements

Integration→Complementary strengths
