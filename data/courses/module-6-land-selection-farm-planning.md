1. Selecting Suitable Land
## 1. Introduction
Land selection is the systematic process of identifying and choosing a piece of land that is suitable for
a particular crop, farming system, livestock enterprise, orchard, greenhouse, or integrated farm.
The important point is that there is no universally “best” land.
A piece of land is suitable only in relation to what you want to produce and how you intend to
manage it.
For example:
- land suitable for wheat may not be ideal for an orchard,
- land suitable for paddy may be unsuitable for crops requiring excellent drainage,
- land suitable for open-field vegetables may not be the best location for a greenhouse,
- land with good soil may still be unsuitable if irrigation water is unavailable or the site floods
frequently.

## 2. Definition
Selecting suitable land is the process of evaluating the physical, climatic, soil, water,
environmental, infrastructural, legal and economic characteristics of a site and
choosing land that can support the intended agricultural activity sustainably and
profitably.
In simple words:
Choose land according to the requirements of the crop and farming system—not
merely according to how the land looks.

3. Why Land Selection Is Important
Land is one of the most important long-term agricultural resources.
A poor land-selection decision can create problems for many years because some characteristics are
expensive or difficult to change.
For example:
- soil texture cannot practically be changed across a large farm,
- slope may require major earthwork,
- poor drainage can require drainage infrastructure,
- saline/sodic conditions can require reclamation,
- shallow soil depth cannot easily be increased economically,
- unreliable irrigation can limit crop choice,
- remote land can increase transport costs.
Therefore:
Correct land selection→Lower production risk→Better resource use→Higher sustainability      

4. Land Selection vs Land Suitability
These terms are related but different.

Land suitability
Land suitability asks:
How appropriate is this land for a particular use?

Land selection
Land selection asks:
Which available land should be chosen for the intended agricultural use?
Therefore:
Land suitability→Land evaluation→Land selection

55. Limiting-Factor Principle
One poor characteristic can sometimes make otherwise good land unsuitable.
For example:
- excellent soil + no water,
- good climate + severe salinity,
- good soil + frequent flooding,
- good land + legal restriction,
- good production potential + impossible market access.
Conceptually:
Overall suitability is constrained by major limiting factors

67. Common Mistakes in Land Selection
Mistake 1 — Selecting land only by appearance
Dark soil does not automatically mean highly suitable soil.

Mistake 2 — Ignoring water quality
A reliable water source can still be saline or sodic.

Mistake 3 — Looking only at annual rainfall
Distribution and timing matter.

Mistake 4 — Ignoring drainage
Good-looking land may become waterlogged.

Mistake 5 — Ignoring soil depth
Surface soil may appear good while bedrock or hardpan is close below.

Mistake 6 — Testing only pH
A complete soil evaluation requires more than pH.

Mistake 7 — Ignoring historical land use
Previous contamination or soil problems may remain.

Mistake 8 — Ignoring market access
Production without an economically viable market can fail.

Mistake 9 — Ignoring legal status
Physical suitability does not guarantee legal suitability.

Mistake 10 — Choosing land based only on current conditions
Future water and climate risks matter.

## 4. Drainage
## 1. Introduction
Drainage is the process by which excess water is removed from the soil or land surface so that the crop
root zone maintains an appropriate balance between water and air.
In agriculture, drainage is not about removing all water from the soil. The objective is to remove excess
water while retaining enough moisture for plant growth.
Good Drainage=Removal of Excess Water+Maintenance of Suitable Root-Zone Moisture  =   +    
This makes drainage an important factor in land selection, crop selection, irrigation management
and soil health.

## 2. Definition
Agricultural drainage is the natural or artificial removal of excess surface water or
subsurface water from agricultural land to maintain suitable soil moisture, aeration
and root-zone conditions for crop growth.
In simple words:
Drainage controls excess water in the field so that plant roots receive enough water
without remaining continuously waterlogged.

3. Why Drainage Is Important
Plant roots require both:
- water, and
- oxygen.
When soil becomes saturated, water occupies most or all of the pore space.
Soil Saturation→Air-filled pores↓→O2↓     O 2  
This can cause:

- poor root respiration,
- reduced root growth,
- nutrient-uptake problems,
- disease,
- poor crop establishment,
- reduced growth,
- yield loss.
Therefore:
Drainage→Root-zone aeration→Root health→Crop productivity

Water”

   Therefore:
Too little drainage→waterlogging

Too much drainage→excessive water loss    
The objective is optimum drainage.

7. Previous Crop
## 1. Introduction
The previous crop is the crop that was grown on a field immediately before the crop currently being
planned or selected.
It is an important factor in land selection, crop planning, crop rotation, nutrient management, pest
and disease management, soil health, and yield prediction.
A field should not be evaluated only by looking at its present condition. The crop grown previously
may have changed the:
- nutrient status,
- soil moisture,
- soil organic matter,
- pest population,
- disease inoculum,
- weed population,
- soil structure,
- residual chemicals,
- nitrogen availability,

- crop residue quantity,
- soil biological activity.
Therefore:
Previous Crop→Soil + Pest + Disease + Nutrient Changes→Suitability of Next Crop     

## 2. Definition
Previous crop is the crop grown on a particular field immediately before the crop
being considered for the next production cycle.
For example:
Wheat→Previous Crop→Soybean      Here, wheat is the previous crop for soybean.

3. Why Previous Crop Matters
The previous crop creates a carry-over effect into the next crop.
The previous crop can influence:
1. soil nutrients,
2. nitrogen availability,
3. organic matter,
4. soil structure,
5. soil moisture,
6. crop residues,
7. weeds,
8. insects,
9. diseases,
10.soil microorganisms,
11.allelopathy,
12.fertilizer requirements,

13.crop establishment,
14.crop yield.
Thus:
Previous Crop→Residual Field Conditions→Next Crop Performance 
   

4. Previous Crop and Crop Rotation
The previous crop is a fundamental part of crop rotation.
Example:
Rice→Wheat→Mung Bean     
Here:
- rice is previous crop for wheat,
- wheat is previous crop for mung bean.
Crop rotation deliberately changes crops over time to manage soil resources and biological risks.

5. Previous Crop vs Cropping System
These terms are different.

Previous crop
The crop immediately before the current crop.

Cropping system
The complete planned arrangement and sequence of crops over time and/or space.
For example:
Rice→Wheat→Mung Bean   The previous crop of wheat is rice.
The entire sequence is a cropping system/rotation.

6. Previous Crop and Soil Nutrients
Different crops remove different quantities of nutrients.

For example:
Crop growth→Nutrient uptake→Harvest→Nutrient removal     Therefore:
Previous Crop→Nutrient Removal→Residual Soil Fertility     
However, nutrient removal depends on:
- crop yield,
- harvested portion,
- residue management,
- crop nutrient concentration,
- fertilizer application.

- soybean,
- pea,

This is one reason legumes can provide rotational benefits to subsequent crops.
Important: nitrogen benefit is not automatically equal to the entire amount of nitrogen fixed by the
legume. Much depends on biomass production, harvest removal, residue management, decomposition
and environmental conditions.

10. Previous Crop and Pest Management
Pests may survive between cropping cycles.
Previous Crop→Host Availability→Pest Survival→Next Crop Risk 
    
Continuous cultivation of a host crop can allow pest populations to persist.
Changing crops can disrupt some pest life cycles.

11. Previous Crop and Disease
Disease pathogens may survive in:
- crop residues,
- soil,
- roots,
- seeds,
- volunteer plants.
Therefore:

Susceptible Previous Crop→Pathogen Carry-over→Disease Risk in Next Crop     
This is particularly important when the next crop is also a host of the same pathogen.

12. Host Continuity
Consider:
Crop A→Crop A   If a pathogen or pest specifically depends on Crop A, continuous cultivation may maintain its host.
But:
Crop A→Non-host Crop   may interrupt the pest or disease cycle.
Therefore crop rotation is an important component of integrated pest and disease management.

13. Previous Crop and Weeds
The previous crop influences weed populations through:
- crop competition,
- herbicide use,
- harvest timing,
- residue cover,
- soil disturbance.
Some weeds may become strongly associated with repeated cropping systems.
Repeated Crop→Repeated Selection Pressure→Certain Weed Populations     
Changing crops can alter:
- planting date,
- canopy structure,
- herbicide options,
- tillage,
- competitive ability.

This can help diversify weed-management pressures.

14. Previous Crop Residues
Crop residues include:
- stems,
- leaves,
- roots,
- pods,
- husks,
- straw,
- other remaining biomass.
Residues can affect the next crop through:
- carbon input,
- nitrogen immobilization,
- nutrient recycling,
- soil cover,
- moisture conservation,
- disease carry-over,
- allelopathy.

15. Residue Decomposition
The basic sequence is:
Crop Residue→Microbial Decomposition→Organic Matter Transformation→Nutrient Recycling d

- temperature,
- moisture,

- residue quality,
- microbial activity.

16. Carbon-to-Nitrogen Ratio
The C:N ratio of residue is important.

High C:N residue
For example, mature cereal straw often has relatively high C:N.
Microbes may require additional available nitrogen for decomposition.
High C:N residue→N Immobilization tendency↑     

Lower C:N residue
Decomposition can generally release nitrogen more readily when other conditions are suitable.
Lower C:N residue→Mineralization tendency↑     
These are tendencies rather than absolute rules.

17. Nitrogen Immobilization
When microbes decompose carbon-rich residues, they may temporarily take up mineral nitrogen.
NH4+, NO3−→Microbial biomass   
This is called nitrogen immobilization.
It can temporarily reduce nitrogen availability to the next crop.

18. Nitrogen Mineralization
Later, decomposition and microbial turnover can release nitrogen.
Organic N→NH4+→NO3−   NH 4 +  NO 3 -  
This is nitrogen mineralization followed by processes such as nitrification.
Therefore residue effects can change over time.

19. Previous Crop and Soil Organic Matter
Leaving crop residues can increase carbon inputs.
Crop Residues→Soil Organic Carbon    
Organic matter can contribute to:
- aggregation,
- water retention,
- nutrient cycling,
- microbial activity,
- soil structure.
However, the actual increase in stable soil organic carbon depends on residue quantity, quality,
decomposition, climate and management.

20. Previous Crop and Soil Structure
Different root systems affect soil differently.
Deep-rooted crops may:
- create channels,
- improve biological pore formation,
- explore deeper layers.
Fibrous-rooted crops may:
- contribute extensive root biomass,
- improve aggregation through root and microbial activity.
Thus:
Previous Crop Root System→Soil Physical Environment→Next Crop Root Growth     

21. Previous Crop and Soil Moisture
The previous crop consumes water.

A crop with high water use can leave the soil relatively depleted if rainfall/irrigation has not
replenished it.
Previous Crop Water Use→Residual Soil Moisture    
Therefore the next crop's establishment can be affected.
This is particularly important in:
- rainfed farming,
- dryland agriculture,
- regions with short growing seasons.

22. Soil Moisture Carry-Over
A simplified water balance is:
ΔS=P+I−R−D−ET 
where:
- ΔS S = change in soil-water storage,
- PP = precipitation,
- II = irrigation,
- RR = runoff,
- DD = drainage,
- ETET = evapotranspiration.
The previous crop affects ETET, and therefore can influence residual soil water.

23. Previous Crop and Soil Compaction

- plough pans,
- subsoil compaction.

Heavy Machinery→Soil Compaction→Root/Water Movement Problems

- tillage,

26. Previous Crop and Allelopathy
Some plants release biochemical compounds that can influence the germination or growth of other
plants.
This phenomenon is called allelopathy.
The sequence can be:
Previous Crop/Residues→Biochemical Compounds→Potential Effect on Next Crop     
Effects can be:
- inhibitory,
- stimulatory,
- crop-specific,
- concentration-dependent.
Allelopathy should not be assumed whenever a crop performs poorly after another crop; other causes
such as nutrients, pathogens, moisture and herbicide residues must also be considered.

## 1. Introduction
Market accessibility refers to how easily and economically a farmer can reach suitable markets and
sell agricultural products at the required time, quantity, quality and price conditions.
It is an important factor in land selection and farm planning because producing a crop is only one
part of farming. The farmer must also be able to move the produce from the farm to buyers, processors,
wholesalers, retailers or other markets.
Farm Production→Transport→Market→Sale→Farm Income 
     
A field can be agronomically suitable but economically unattractive if access to markets is extremely
difficult or costly.

## 2. Definition
Market accessibility is the degree to which farmers can conveniently, reliably and
economically access appropriate markets for purchasing inputs and selling
agricultural outputs.

- irrigation equipment,
- fuel.

Irrigation Layout
## 1. Introduction
Irrigation layout is the planned arrangement of the water source, pumping system, mainlines, submainlines, laterals, outlets, control structures and field application points so that irrigation water
can be distributed efficiently and uniformly to the crop root zone.
In simple terms:
Irrigation layout answers: Where does the water come from, how does it travel, where
is it distributed, and how does it reach each crop?
The basic flow is:
Water Source→Pump→Mainline→Sub-main→Laterals→Emitters/Sprinklers→Root Zone         
The exact arrangement depends on the farm size, field shape, slope, soil, crop, irrigation method,
water source, available pressure and water requirement.

## 2. Definition
Irrigation layout is the spatial arrangement and hydraulic organization of an
irrigation system used to convey and distribute water from its source to different
fields or crop zones at the required rate and pressure.
It includes both:

Physical arrangement
Where pipes, channels, pumps and outlets are located.

Hydraulic arrangement
How much water flows through each component and at what pressure/head.

3. Objectives of Irrigation Layout
A good irrigation layout aims to provide:

- adequate water,
- uniform distribution,
- appropriate application rate,
- efficient conveyance,
- minimum unnecessary water loss,
- suitable operating pressure,

75. Exam-Ready Answer
Drainage layout is the planned spatial arrangement of surface and subsurface drains,
collectors, main drains, outlets and associated structures used to collect, convey and
safely remove excess water from agricultural land. Its primary objective is to maintain
suitable soil moisture and aeration in the crop root zone rather than to remove all soil
water. Drainage layout depends on rainfall, irrigation, soil texture and structure,
infiltration and hydraulic conductivity, slope, topography, water-table depth, field
size, crop requirements and the availability of a safe drainage outlet. Surface drainage
uses channels, ditches, grading and related structures to remove excess surface water,
while subsurface drainage uses buried perforated pipes or similar systems to control
excess subsurface water and shallow water tables. Drainage should be planned
together with irrigation, roads, farm layout and erosion-control measures. Proper
drainage can reduce waterlogging, improve root aeration and trafficability, and
support crop growth, while excessive drainage can increase nutrient losses and
downstream environmental impacts.

74. Exam-Ready Answer
Storage planning is the systematic process of determining what agricultural products
- r farm materials need to be stored, the quantity and duration of storage, the required
environmental conditions, the type and capacity of storage facility, its location,
handling system, protection measures and monitoring system. It is important for
minimizing quantitative and qualitative post-harvest losses and maintaining the

quality, safety and value of agricultural products. Storage planning considers
moisture, temperature, relative humidity, ventilation, insects, rodents,
microorganisms, mechanical damage, flooding, accessibility and storage duration.
Different commodities require different systems: cereals and pulses commonly require
dry storage with moisture and pest control, seeds require conditions that preserve
viability and vigor, while fruits and vegetables may require temperature- and
humidity-controlled storage and an appropriate cold chain. Storage facilities should
be integrated with farm roads, harvesting, processing, drainage and market-access
systems. Economic factors such as construction, energy, labor, maintenance, expected
losses and market conditions should also be considered.
