2. Definition of Agriculture
General definition
Agriculture is the science, art, business, and technology of cultivating plants and raising animals
for human use.

Scientific definition
Agriculture can be understood as the controlled management of plants, animals, soil, water, climate,
nutrients, and other biological resources to obtain useful agricultural products.

Simple definition
Agriculture means producing useful products from plants and animals through
planned management of land, water, nutrients, organisms, labor, and technology.

3. Meaning of the Word Agriculture
The word agriculture comes from Latin:
- Ager = field or land
- Cultura = cultivation or tending
Therefore:
Agriculture = cultivation/tending of land
Historically, the term primarily referred to cultivating land, but its modern meaning has expanded to
include the entire agricultural production system.

8. Agricultural Production System
Agriculture can be represented as a system:
Natural resources + Inputs + Management + Biological processes → Agricultural products
For crop production:
Seed + Soil + Water + Nutrients + Light + Suitable temperature + Management → Crop growth
→ Yield
The final yield depends on the interaction of genetics × environment × management.
A useful conceptual model is:
Yield = f(Genotype, Environment, Management)
where:
- Genotype = genetic characteristics of the crop
- Environment = soil, climate, water, etc.
- Management = farmer's production practices

Based on water availability

Based on production objective
Subsistence agriculture
Primarily intended to meet the producer's household needs.

Commercial agriculture

Based on production intensity
Extensive agriculture
Large areas with relatively lower input intensity per unit area.

Intensive agriculture
Higher levels of inputs and management are used to obtain higher production from a given area.

Based on production philosophy
Conventional agriculture
Generally uses modern synthetic inputs and standardized production practices.

Organic agriculture
Uses management systems emphasizing organic inputs and ecological processes and follows applicable
- rganic standards.

Natural farming
Uses approaches centered on biological/ecological processes and minimizing externally synthesized
inputs, with definitions varying among systems.

Sustainable agriculture
Attempts to maintain agricultural productivity while conserving natural resources and reducing
undesirable environmental impacts.

Regenerative agriculture
A broad term used for approaches intended to improve soil health, biodiversity and ecosystem
functions while producing agricultural outputs. Practices and definitions vary.

10. Crop Production Cycle

Field selection → Soil testing → Land preparation → Variety selection → Seed selection → Seed

Not every crop follows exactly the same sequence.

21. Key Principle of Agriculture
The central objective of agriculture is not simply:
"Produce maximum yield."
A complete agricultural system attempts to achieve an appropriate balance among:
Yield + Quality + Profitability + Resource efficiency + Environmental sustainability + Long-term
productivity
The optimum balance depends on the crop, location, farmer, market, climate and production system.

- Agriculture is the science, art, technology and business of managing biological and natural

Core takeaway
Types of agriculture are classifications of agricultural systems based on factors such

Crop vs Plant vs Variety vs Cultivar
These four terms are closely related, but they describe different levels of agricultural classification.
Understanding their hierarchy is extremely important because farmers, agronomists, seed companies,
researchers, and agricultural databases use these terms differently.

## 1. Plant
Definition
A plant is an individual living organism belonging to the plant kingdom.
In agriculture, a plant is the individual biological organism growing in a field, garden, orchard,
greenhouse, forest, or other environment.

Examples
One individual:
- Rice plant
- Wheat plant
- Mango plant

- Tomato plant
- Cotton plant
If a farmer has 10,000 individual wheat plants in a field, each one is a plant.

Basic hierarchy
A plant can be described scientifically through biological classification:
Kingdom → Division → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species → Individual plant
For example:
Plant → Wheat plant → Triticum → Triticum aestivum → individual wheat plant

## 2. Crop
Definition
A crop is a plant or group of plants that is cultivated and managed by humans for a particular
useful purpose, usually food, feed, fiber, oil, medicine, fuel, or industrial raw material.
The key difference is:
Plant = biological organism
Crop = plant cultivated for a purpose

Examples
- Rice crop
- Wheat crop
- Cotton crop
- Soybean crop
- Tomato crop
- Sugarcane crop

A crop usually refers to the cultivated production system, rather than one individual plant.

Example
Suppose a farmer plants 50,000 rice plants in a field.
- One individual = rice plant
- The cultivated population in the field = rice crop
- The crop belongs to a particular species and variety/cultivar.

3. Crop Does Not Always Mean One Species
The word crop is primarily an agricultural term, not a strict botanical taxonomic rank.
For example, "cereal crops" includes several species:
- Wheat
- Rice
- Maize
- Barley
- Oats
- Sorghum
Similarly, "oilseed crops" include:
- Soybean
- Groundnut
- Mustard
- Sunflower
- Sesame

Therefore, crop is a functional/agricultural classification, not a botanical classification.

## 4. Variety
General meaning
A variety is a group of plants within a species that has distinguishable characteristics.
In agriculture, the word is commonly used for a cultivated type that differs from other types in
characteristics such as:
- Yield
- Plant height
- Maturity duration
- Grain/fruit characteristics
- Disease resistance
- Pest resistance
- Drought tolerance
- Temperature tolerance
- Quality
- Color
- Shape

Example
Rice may have many varieties/types with different:
- Grain lengths
- Maturity periods
- Yield potential

- Disease resistance
- Water requirements
- Cooking qualities

5. Important Problem With the Word "Variety"
Variety is a context-dependent term.
In everyday farming and seed marketing, people often use:
variety = a particular type of crop
For example:
"Which rice variety are you growing?"
But in formal botanical/taxonomic terminology, variety (var.) can also refer to a specific taxonomic
rank below species.
These two usages should not automatically be treated as identical.

## 6. Cultivar
Definition
Cultivar means a cultivated variety: a plant grouping that has been deliberately selected or developed
by humans and is maintained in cultivation because of particular desirable characteristics.
The word comes from:
cultivated + variety → cultivar
The term is especially important in horticulture, plant breeding and taxonomy.

Examples
Cultivars can be selected for:

- Higher yield
- Fruit quality
- Flower color
- Disease resistance
- Uniform maturity
- Plant architecture
- Taste
- Storage ability
- Processing quality

7. How Cultivar Is Different From Botanical
Variety
This distinction is very important.

Botanical variety
A botanical variety is a formal taxonomic rank below species.
It is written:
var.
For example, a scientific name may contain a botanical variety designation.

Cultivar
A cultivar is a cultivated plant group recognized and maintained because of characteristics selected by
humans.
Cultivar names are generally written in single quotation marks after the scientific name.
Example format:

Mangifera indica 'Alphonso'
Here:
- Mangifera = genus
- indica = species epithet
- Mangifera indica = species name
- 'Alphonso' = cultivar name

conditions are properly identified.

22. Common Mistakes
Mistake 1
"A crop is one plant."

❌ Incorrect.

A crop generally refers to a cultivated plant population/production system.

Mistake 2
"Every plant is a crop."

❌ Incorrect.

A wild plant is still a plant but isn't necessarily a crop.

Mistake 3
"Variety and species are the same."

❌ Incorrect.

Species is a biological taxonomic category; "variety" has different meanings depending on context and
can refer to a lower taxonomic rank or a cultivated type in agricultural usage.

Mistake 4
"Every variety is a cultivar."

❌ Not necessarily.

In informal agricultural language they are frequently treated as equivalent, but technically the terms
have distinct meanings.

Mistake 5
"Cultivar and hybrid mean the same thing."

❌ Incorrect.

A cultivar describes a cultivated plant group; hybrid describes offspring produced from a cross.

Annual, Biennial and Perennial Crops
The terms annual, biennial, and perennial describe the life cycle or lifespan of a crop plant—
specifically, how long an individual plant normally survives and how many growing seasons it takes to
complete its reproductive life cycle.
Understanding this classification is important for crop planning, sowing, harvesting, irrigation,
nutrient management, pruning, propagation, and farm economics.

1. Annual Crops
Definition
An annual crop is a crop whose plant normally completes its entire life cycle within one growing
season or one year, from seed germination through vegetative growth, reproduction, seed production,
and death.

Basic life cycle
Seed → Germination → Seedling → Vegetative growth → Flowering → Pollination →
Fertilization → Fruit/seed development → Maturity → Seed production → Plant death
The next crop is normally established from new seed.

Examples
Common annual crops include:
- Rice
- Wheat
- Maize
- Barley
- Chickpea
- Lentil
- Green gram
- Black gram
- Soybean
- Groundnut
- Mustard

- Sunflower
- Sesame
- Cotton
- Tomato
- Potato
- Many annual vegetables
Important: Some crops commonly treated as annuals in agriculture can biologically behave differently
under favorable climates or production systems. Classification should therefore consider the species
and production context.

2. Characteristics of Annual Crops
1. Short life cycle
They complete their reproductive cycle relatively quickly.

2. Usually planted every season
After harvest, farmers generally establish a new crop.

3. Rapid growth
Many annual crops have evolved or are selected to complete their life cycle quickly.

4. Seed production is important
The plant usually flowers and produces seeds before completing its life cycle.

5. Suitable for crop rotation
Because they are replanted frequently, annual crops are particularly useful in:
- Crop rotation
- Multiple cropping

- Intercropping
- Sequential cropping

3. Types of Annual Crops
Annual crops can be grouped according to the season in which they are grown.

Kharif / rainy-season crops
In much of India, examples include:
- Rice
- Maize
- Soybean
- Groundnut
- Cotton
- Sorghum

Examples include:
- Wheat
- Chickpea
- Mustard
- Lentil
- Barley

Zaid / summer crops
Depending on region and irrigation availability:

- Watermelon
- Muskmelon
- Cucumber
- Some vegetables
- Short-duration pulses
The exact crop calendar varies by location.

4. Biennial Crops
Definition
A biennial crop normally requires two growing seasons or approximately two years to complete its
life cycle.
The plant typically has two major phases.

First year
Seed → Germination → Vegetative growth → Storage-organ/root development → Dormancy or
- verwintering

Second year
Resumption of growth → Flowering → Pollination → Seed formation → Maturity → Plant death

5. Examples of Biennial Crops
Common examples include:
- Carrot
- Beetroot
- Radish

- Turnip
- Cabbage
- Onion
- Parsley
However, whether a particular crop behaves as a true biennial can depend on genotype, temperature,
photoperiod, management and production system.

6. Important Feature of Biennial Crops
During the first year, many biennial plants primarily develop vegetative structures and storage organs.
Examples:

Carrot
First phase:
Root development → carbohydrate storage
Second phase:
Flowering → seed production

Onion
First phase:
Leaf and bulb development
Second phase:
Flower stalk → flowers → seeds
This is why farmers often harvest some biennial crops during their first-year vegetative/storage stage,
before they complete their natural reproductive life cycle.

7. Vernalization and Biennial Crops
Some biennial plants require exposure to a period of low temperature to transition from vegetative
growth to reproductive development.
This process is called:
Vernalization
Vernalization can promote flowering in plants that have a temperature requirement for reproductive
development.
This is particularly important in understanding:
- Cabbage
- Carrot
- Beet
- Some onion types
- Other cool-season crops

8. Perennial Crops
Definition
A perennial crop is a crop whose plants live for more than two years and can survive through
multiple growing seasons.
Instead of dying after one reproductive cycle, the plant can continue to grow and reproduce for several
years.

General life cycle
Establishment → Vegetative growth → Reproductive growth → Harvest → Regrowth →
Reproduction → Harvest → Regrowth → ...
The cycle can continue for many years.

Important: Perennial classification depends on the biological species and production system. Some

10. Characteristics of Perennial Crops
Long lifespan
Plants remain productive for multiple years.

Repeated harvest
Many perennial crops can be harvested repeatedly without replanting after every harvest.

Established root system
Perennial plants can develop substantial root systems and persistent underground structures.

Lower frequency of establishment
Farmers don't normally need to establish a new plant population every year.

Long-term management
Management may include:
- Pruning
- Training
- Irrigation
- Fertilization
- Pest management
- Disease management
- Canopy management
- Weed management
- Harvest management

Plants survive for multiple years but generally do not develop a large permanent woody trunk.
Examples:
- Alfalfa
- Some forage grasses

Examples:
- Mango

- Guava

Persistent underground structures

- Rhizomes

14. Why Do Annual Plants Die After One Cycle?
Annual plants have evolved or been selected to prioritize rapid completion of their reproductive
cycle.
The plant:
Germinates → grows → reproduces → produces seeds → completes its life cycle
After successful reproduction, the plant generally senesces and dies.
This strategy allows the species to survive through its seeds.

15. Why Do Biennial Plants Need Two Years?
Biennial plants divide their life cycle into two major stages.

Stage 1 — Resource accumulation
The plant develops:
- Leaves
- Roots
- Bulbs
- Storage organs

- Carbohydrate reserves

Stage 2 — Reproduction
After receiving appropriate environmental signals, the plant:
- Produces a flowering stalk
- Flowers
- Produces seeds
- Completes its life cycle
This allows the plant to accumulate enough resources before reproduction.

16. Why Do Perennial Plants Survive for Years?
Perennial plants possess adaptations that allow them to survive unfavorable periods and resume growth.
These can include:
- Persistent roots
- Woody stems
- Crowns
- Rhizomes
- Bulbs
- Dormant buds
- Stored carbohydrates
- Protective bark
- Dormancy mechanisms
Instead of restarting completely from seed every year, the established plant survives and regenerates.

## 17. Dormancy
Dormancy is a period during which plant growth and/or development is temporarily reduced or
suspended.
It can help plants survive unfavorable conditions such as:
- Winter
- Drought
- Heat
- Short photoperiod
- Other environmental stresses
Dormancy is especially important in many perennial plants.

18. Crop Duration vs Life Cycle
These concepts should not be confused.

Crop duration
The time from establishment/sowing until the farmer's intended harvest.

Biological life cycle
The complete sequence from:
Birth/establishment → growth → reproduction → completion of life cycle
For example, a farmer may harvest a plant before it completes its biological reproductive cycle.
Therefore:
Harvest duration ≠ necessarily complete biological lifespan

19. Important Example: Carrot
Carrot is commonly classified as a biennial.

First year
- Germination
- Leaf development
- Taproot enlargement
- Storage of carbohydrates
The farmer generally harvests the root during this stage.

Second year
If left in the field:
- The plant resumes growth
- Produces a flowering stalk
- Flowers develop
- Seeds form
- The plant completes its life cycle
Thus:
Commercial harvest can occur before the crop completes its natural biological life
cycle.

20. Important Example: Onion
Onion is commonly treated as a biennial in its natural reproductive cycle.

First season
Seed → seedling → leaves → bulb formation
The bulb is commonly harvested.

Second season
If the bulb is replanted or retained:
Regrowth → flower stalk → flowering → seed production
Therefore, onion can be cultivated for bulb production even though its natural reproductive life cycle
extends into a second season.

21. Important Example: Sugarcane
Sugarcane is generally considered a perennial grass, but its commercial management can differ.
A planted crop is called:
Plant crop
After harvesting, the remaining underground portions can produce another crop:
Ratoon crop
Thus:
Plant crop → harvest → ratoon → harvest → ratoon
for several cycles depending on variety, environment and management.
This demonstrates why biological lifespan and commercial crop cycle are not always the same
thing.

22. Important Example: Mango
Mango is a perennial woody fruit crop.

A mango tree can:
- Establish
- Grow vegetatively
- Flower
- Produce fruit
- Be harvested
- Continue growing
- Flower again in subsequent years
Therefore:
One mango tree can produce multiple crops over many years.

23. Annual, Biennial and Perennial —
Agricultural Importance
The life-cycle category affects almost every farming decision.

Land preparation
Annual crops generally require field preparation before each crop cycle.
Perennial crops usually require major land preparation mainly during establishment, followed by longterm management.

Irrigation
Annual crops generally have irrigation schedules linked to one crop season.
Perennial crops require long-term irrigation management and may have changing water requirements
- ver the year.

Fertilization
Annual crops often receive nutrient applications according to a specific seasonal crop calendar.
Perennials require:
- Establishment fertilization
- Maintenance fertilization
- Stage-specific nutrient management
- Long-term soil fertility management

Weed management
Annual crops often require weed management during the crop season.
Perennial systems may require weed management repeatedly over several years.

Pest and disease management
Annual crops may face season-specific pest and disease cycles.
Perennial crops can provide a continuous or recurring habitat for certain pests and pathogens, requiring
long-term monitoring.

Farm economics
Annual
- Frequent planting
- Frequent crop decisions
- Shorter production cycle

- Faster return in many systems

Biennial
- Longer production cycle
- Two-stage management

Perennial
- Higher establishment investment may occur
- Delayed initial returns are common for many tree crops
- Long productive lifespan can provide repeated returns

24. Advantages and Limitations
Annual crops
Advantages
- Quick production cycle
- Flexible crop rotation
- Easy to change crops between seasons
- Suitable for multiple cropping
- Faster response to changing market conditions

Limitations

- Repeated land preparation may be required
- Greater dependence on timely seasonal operations
- Soil disturbance can be significant depending on management

Biennial crops
Advantages
- Useful storage organs can develop during the vegetative phase

Perennial crops
Advantages
- Repeated production without annual re-establishment
- Long-term utilization of land
- Established root systems
- Potential soil and ecosystem benefits in appropriate systems
- Can provide long-term farm income

Limitations
- Initial establishment can be expensive
- Returns may be delayed
- Long-term pest and disease management is required

- Changing land use can be more difficult
- Poor cultivar/site selection can have long-lasting consequences

Annual, biennial and perennial are classifications based on the biological life cycle of the plant:

Kharif, Rabi and Zaid Cropping Seasons
In Indian agriculture, crops are commonly classified according to the season in which they are sown
and grown. The three major agricultural seasons are:
1. Kharif season — mainly associated with the monsoon
2. Rabi season — mainly associated with the winter
3. Zaid season — mainly the short summer season between Rabi and Kharif
These are cropping-season classifications, not botanical classifications. The exact sowing and
harvesting dates vary by state, climate, crop variety, irrigation availability, and local farming
practices.

1. Kharif Season
Definition
Kharif crops are crops generally sown with the arrival of the southwest monsoon and grown during
the warm and rainy part of the year.
In much of India:
Sowing: approximately June–July
Growing period: monsoon and post-monsoon
Harvesting: approximately September–November
The exact period differs considerably across regions.

Cropping Systems
## 1. Introduction
A cropping system is the way one or more crops are planned, arranged, grown, and managed on a
piece of land over a particular period of time.
It describes not just which crop is grown, but also:
- how many crops are grown,
- whether they are grown alone or together,
- the sequence in which they are grown,

- how often the land is cropped,
- how crops are spatially arranged,
- how crops interact with soil, water, nutrients, pests and climate,
- and how crop residues and resources are managed.

Cropping pattern = what crops are distributed where.

Subsistence vs Commercial Farming
## 1. Introduction
Farming is the practice of producing crops, livestock, or other agricultural products through the use of
land, labor, water, inputs, technology, and management.
Based primarily on the purpose of production, farming can broadly be classified into:
1. Subsistence farming
2. Commercial farming

The fundamental difference is:
Subsistence farming primarily aims to meet the farmer's household or local
consumption needs, whereas commercial farming primarily aims to produce
agricultural products for sale in markets and generate income.
This is a purpose-based classification, not a strict classification based on farm size. A small farm can
be commercial, and a larger farm can produce partly for household consumption.

2. Subsistence Farming
Definition
Subsistence farming is a farming system in which agricultural production is primarily intended to
satisfy the food and basic livelihood requirements of the farmer and household, with relatively
limited production intended for market sale.
The farmer's main objective is generally:
Produce enough for household needs first.
Any surplus may be sold, exchanged, stored, or used for other purposes.

4. Types of Subsistence Farming
Subsistence farming is not one single system.

A. Primitive Subsistence Farming
This generally refers to farming systems using relatively simple tools and low levels of external inputs.
Examples historically or in some traditional contexts include:
- shifting cultivation
- certain forms of nomadic or semi-nomadic subsistence production
Production is strongly dependent on natural conditions.

B. Intensive Subsistence Farming
In intensive subsistence farming, relatively small areas of land may support high levels of production
through intensive use of:
- labor
- irrigation
- organic manure
- fertilizers
- careful crop management
Rice-based systems in densely populated regions are often discussed as examples of intensive
subsistence agriculture, although many such systems are now also strongly commercialized.

Important point
Intensive does not mean commercial.
"Intensive" describes the level of resource use per unit of land, whereas "subsistence" describes the
primary production objective.

5. Advantages of Subsistence Farming
1. Household food security
The farmer produces food directly for consumption.

2. Reduced dependence on food markets
Some household food requirements can be met without purchasing everything from the market.

3. Crop diversity
Growing multiple crops can provide nutritional diversity.

4. Risk diversification
If one crop fails, other crops or livestock may still provide food.

5. Local resource use
Traditional systems may make substantial use of:
- farmyard manure
- crop residues
- saved seed
- family labor

6. Livelihood support
It can provide employment and basic livelihood for farming households.

6. Limitations of Subsistence Farming

Lower purchasing power
If farm income is low, it can be difficult to purchase:
- machinery
- improved seed
- fertilizers
- irrigation equipment
- education
- healthcare
- other necessities

Exposure to climate risk
Drought, floods, pests and diseases can directly threaten household food availability.

Limited access to technology
Smallholders may face difficulty accessing:
- machinery
- credit
- storage
- quality inputs
- extension services

Post-harvest losses
Limited storage and processing facilities can result in losses.

Organic, natural, conventional, and integrated farming are different approaches to managing
agricultural production.
They differ mainly in:
- How nutrients are supplied
- How pests and diseases are managed
- How external inputs are used
- How soil health is maintained

- How crops, livestock, trees, and other farm components are combined
- The degree of dependence on synthetic inputs
- The importance given to ecological processes
A very important point is that these terms are not all mutually exclusive in exactly the same way.
They describe different farming philosophies and management systems.
For example:
A farm can be integrated + conventional, or integrated + organic, depending on how
integration and input rules are defined.

principles and standards, emphasizing ecological processes, biodiversity, recycling of organic

Organic systems commonly rely heavily on biological and organic nutrient sources such as:

Important misconception
Organic does not mean:
"No chemicals exist."
Everything is composed of chemicals at the molecular level.
The meaningful distinction is that organic agriculture uses specific permitted/prohibited inputs and
processes according to applicable standards, rather than simply defining farming as "chemical-free."

6. Advantages of Organic Farming
Soil health
Organic matter management can improve:
- soil structure
- water-holding capacity
- biological activity

Biodiversity
Diversified systems can support ecological diversity.

Reduced dependence on synthetic agricultural inputs
Organic standards restrict many synthetic inputs.

Recycling
Farm wastes can become useful resources.

Potential environmental benefits
Well-managed organic systems can reduce certain environmental pressures, although outcomes depend
- n the whole farming system.

7. Limitations of Organic Farming
Nutrient availability
Nutrients from organic materials may become available more slowly and depend on decomposition.

Nutrient concentration
Large amounts of organic material may be required to supply nutrients.

Weed management
Without many conventional herbicides, farmers may need greater dependence on:
- mechanical control
- mulching
- crop rotation
- manual labor
- competitive crops

Certification and compliance
Certified organic production requires following applicable standards and maintaining records.

Yield variability
Organic yields vary significantly by:
- crop
- location

- soil
- management
- transition stage
- input availability
Therefore, it is incorrect to say that organic farming always produces lower or higher yields.

8. Natural Farming
Definition
Natural farming is an approach that seeks to reduce dependence on externally purchased agricultural
inputs and strengthen natural/ecological processes within the farm ecosystem.
It commonly emphasizes:
- soil biological activity
- biodiversity
- recycling
- mulching
- ecological pest management
- locally available resources
- reduced external input dependence
The exact practices associated with "natural farming" can vary between programs and regions.

- mechanization
- chemical or biological crop protection
- precision agriculture
- modern machinery
- controlled-environment technologies
"Conventional" does not mean that every farm uses every one of these inputs.

- crop + poultry

- crop + dairy
- crop + livestock + fish
- crop + agroforestry

Integrated

Focuses on production standards and ecological management, with defined restrictions on inputs.
